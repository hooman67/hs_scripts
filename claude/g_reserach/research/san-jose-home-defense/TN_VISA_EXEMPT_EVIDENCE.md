# Evidence that a visa-exempt Canadian TN admission is not admission under a nonimmigrant visa

**Verified:** 2026-10-14

No government source makes an individualized finding about Hooman's particular entry. The conclusion follows from combining the controlling DOJ/ATF interpretation with the Canadian visa-exemption and TN-admission regulations. It remains conditional on the factual premise that the most recent admission really occurred without a visa and that there was no later re-entry under a different document.

## The three different meanings of “resident”

The legal systems use “resident” for different purposes. They do not substitute for one another.

| Question | Governing test | Effect here |
|---|---|---|
| Was the Canadian admitted **under a nonimmigrant visa**? | Actual basis/document used for the most recent admission; 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(5)(B) and the DOJ/ATF rule | A visa-exempt Canadian TN admission remains visa-exempt even if the person later lives and works in California. |
| Is the person a **California resident for federal firearm transactions**? | Physical presence in California plus intent to make a home there; 27 CFR § 478.11 | A person living and working in a San Jose home can be a California resident while remaining a TN nonimmigrant. |
| Is the person a **U.S. resident for tax purposes**? | IRS green-card or substantial-presence test, subject to exceptions, elections, and treaties | This determines federal tax treatment. It does not create a visa, permanent residence, or a different answer to Form 4473 question 21.m.1. |

### ATF expressly distinguishes a visitor from an alien worker who makes a home

**Exact official link:**

https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-27/section-478.11#p-478.11(State%20of%20residence)

**Where to look:** the definition **State of residence**, especially Examples 3 and 4.

ATF defines residence as follows:

> An individual resides in a State if he or she is present in a State with the intention of making a home in that State.

Example 3 says an alien on a three-week vacation is not a state resident because the alien does not intend to make a home there. Example 4 says:

> A, an alien, travels to the United States to work for three years in State X. A rents a home in State X, moves his personal possessions into the home, and his family resides with him in the home. A intends to reside in State X during the 3-year period of his employment. A is a resident of State X.

This is strong direct support that a TN worker can be a California resident for a firearm transaction even though TN remains a temporary, nonimmigrant classification.

### TN is a work classification, not a visitor classification

USCIS says TN permits temporary entry to engage in professional business activities and requires a prearranged full-time or part-time job for a U.S. employer or entity:

https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/temporary-workers/tn-usmca-professionals

A Canadian who comes only for tourism is ordinarily a visa-exempt B-2 visitor; someone coming for limited business-visitor activity may be admitted B-1. Describing someone as a “visitor under TN” mixes separate classifications. A Canadian TN entrant is a temporary professional worker.

For the visa-specific firearm prohibition, however, the decisive question remains whether the person was actually admitted using a nonimmigrant visa. DOJ rejected the broader theory that every person in a nonimmigrant classification is covered. On Federal Register page 33627, DOJ's Office of Legal Counsel said the statute applies to only a subset of nonimmigrants—those who possess a nonimmigrant visa.

### Tax residence is separate

The IRS says a noncitizen is a U.S. resident for tax purposes if the person meets the green-card test or substantial-presence test, subject to special rules:

https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/determining-an-individuals-tax-residency-status

The substantial-presence test is described here:

https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/substantial-presence-test

Tax residence does not make a person a lawful permanent resident for immigration or firearm law. It is also unnecessary to establish firearm-law state residence when the facts already satisfy 27 CFR § 478.11. A tax document may sometimes help document an address, but the tax classification itself does not determine Form 4473 question 21.m.1.

## 1. DOJ/ATF says the firearm prohibition does not apply to nonimmigrants who entered without a visa

**Exact official link:**

https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2012/06/07/2012-13762/firearms-disabilities-for-certain-nonimmigrant-aliens-2001r-332p

**Where to look:**

- At the top, under **SUMMARY**, Federal Register page **33625**. Search the page for `lawfully entered the United States without a visa`.
- Under **V. The Present Final Rule**, Federal Register page **33627**. Search for `Nonimmigrant aliens lawfully admitted`.
- On page **33628**, search for `would not need to avail themselves`.

The Summary says:

> The Department of Justice has now determined that the relevant statutory prohibitions on transfer and possession of firearms and ammunition apply only to nonimmigrant aliens who were admitted to the United States under a nonimmigrant visa, and that the prohibitions do not apply to nonimmigrant aliens who lawfully entered the United States without a visa.

Section V says:

> Nonimmigrant aliens lawfully admitted to the United States without a visa, pursuant either to the Visa Waiver Program or other exemptions from visa requirements, will not be prohibited from shipping, transporting, receiving, or possessing firearms or ammunition, and the regulations will also no longer proscribe the sale or other disposition of firearms or ammunition to such nonimmigrant aliens.

Page 33628 says:

> Moreover, nonimmigrants admitted to the United States who did not require a visa are no longer considered to be prohibited, and accordingly they would not need to avail themselves of the exceptions under 18 U.S.C. 922(y)(2) or the waiver under section 922(y)(3).

This is the clearest direct federal authority for the firearm-law proposition.

## 2. Federal regulation says Canadians generally do not require a visa

**Exact official link to 8 CFR § 212.1(a)(1):**

https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/section-212.1#p-212.1(a)(1)

**Where to look:** subsection **(a)(1), Canadian citizens**. Search for `A visa is generally not required`.

It says:

> A visa is generally not required for Canadian citizens, except those Canadians that fall under nonimmigrant visa categories E, K, S, or V ...

TN is not one of the listed exceptions.

## 3. The TN regulation contrasts Mexican visa admission with Canadian port-of-entry admission

**Exact official links:**

- Mexican TN rule, 8 CFR § 214.6(d)(1): https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/section-214.6#p-214.6(d)(1)
- Canadian TN rule, 8 CFR § 214.6(d)(2): https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/section-214.6#p-214.6(d)(2)
- Documentation rule, § 214.6(d)(3): https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/section-214.6#p-214.6(d)(3)

**Where to look:** subsection **(d), Classification of citizens of Canada or Mexico as TN professionals**.

The Mexican provision expressly requires:

> a valid passport and valid TN nonimmigrant visa

The Canadian provision instead says:

> A citizen of Canada ... shall make application for admission with a Department officer at the United States Class A port-of-entry ...

Subsection (d)(3) then distinguishes an application for a visa at a consular office from:

> the case of a citizen of Canada making application for admission at a port-of-entry

That is the regulatory mechanism for a Canadian to be admitted in TN classification without being admitted under a visa.

## 4. USCIS says Canadian TN applicants do not apply for a TN visa

**Exact official link:**

https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/temporary-workers/tn-usmca-professionals

**Where to look:** heading **Canadian Citizens**. Search for `not required to apply for a TN visa`.

It says:

> If you are a Canadian citizen residing outside the United States, you are not required to apply for a TN visa at a U.S. consulate. You may establish eligibility for TN classification at the time you seek admission to the United States ...

## 5. Current ATF Form 4473 expressly tells visa-exempt aliens how to answer

**Exact official form link:**

https://www.atf.gov/firearms/docs/4473-part-1-firearms-transaction-record-over-counter-atf-form-53009/download

**Where to look:** PDF page **5 of 7**, under **Question 21.m. Immigration Status**. Search the PDF for `regulations otherwise exempting them from visa requirements`.

It says:

> Permanent resident aliens and aliens legally admitted to the United States pursuant to either the Visa Waiver Program or to regulations otherwise exempting them from visa requirements may answer “no” to this question, leave 21.m.2 blank and are not required to submit the additional documentation under question 26.d.

Question 21.m.1 asks whether the person was admitted under a nonimmigrant **visa**, rather than merely whether the person holds a nonimmigrant status or classification.

## 6. The statute uses the same visa-specific language

**18 U.S.C. § 922:**

https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title18-section922&num=0&edition=prelim

**Where to look:** subsection **(g)(5)(B)**. Search for `admitted to the United States under a nonimmigrant visa`.

**Definition of nonimmigrant visa, 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(26):**

https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1101&num=0&edition=prelim

**Where to look:** subsection **(a)(26)**. It defines “nonimmigrant visa” as a visa properly issued to an eligible nonimmigrant by a competent officer under the immigration laws.

This wording is why having TN **status** does not automatically mean admission under a TN **visa**.

## 7. Why the later I-797A does not itself supply a visa

**USCIS I-797 types:**

https://www.uscis.gov/forms/filing-guidance/form-i-797-types-and-functions

**Where to look:** the table row for **I-797A**. It says I-797A is issued as a `replacement Form I-94`.

**USCIS Form I-129 page:**

https://www.uscis.gov/i-129

**Where to look:** heading **Evidence of Petition Approval Needed When Traveling**. Search for `Evidence of a petition approval is not a visa`.

It says:

> Evidence of a petition approval is not a visa, and the beneficiary must possess the appropriate nonimmigrant visa (if one is required) before applying for admission into the United States.

The TN extension regulation also preserves the distinction between a person who must seek a visa and one who remains visa-exempt:

https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/section-214.6#p-214.6(h)(1)(ii)

Search for `or, if visa exempt`.

## Resulting evidence chain

1. DOJ/ATF says § 922's nonimmigrant-visa firearm disability does not cover a nonimmigrant lawfully admitted without a visa.
2. 8 CFR § 212.1(a)(1) says Canadians generally do not need visas, and TN is not an exception.
3. 8 CFR § 214.6(d) and USCIS describe Canadian TN admission at the port of entry without a consular TN visa.
4. Current Form 4473 says a person admitted under regulations exempting them from visa requirements may answer question 21.m.1 “No.”
5. USCIS says an I-797A is a replacement I-94 and petition approval is not a visa.
6. 27 CFR § 478.11 independently treats an alien who comes to work for three years and makes a home in a state as a resident of that state.

Therefore, **if** the most recent admission was the visa-exempt Canadian TN admission described, the federal sources support the conclusion that it was not admission “under a nonimmigrant visa.” Because a false Form 4473 answer has criminal consequences, the individual's firearm lawyer should still confirm that the complete travel and status history satisfies that factual premise.
