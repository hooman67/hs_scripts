## Decision and scope

Build a modest but broad household medicine reserve for occasional illnesses and minor injuries during approximately 90 days of disrupted pharmacy access. Cover fever/pain, dehydration/diarrhea, nausea, constipation, allergies, superficial burns, skin problems and eye comfort/irrigation. The user has an existing first-aid/bleeding kit and wants bleeding supplies deferred; this batch neither audits nor endorses the trauma kit. Existing prescriptions stay outside the shopping count. Humans take precedence; veterinary information is separate.

The household currently includes a newborn and toddler. Exact child ages/weights and private health details are retained in chat, not reproduced here. This is a product-selection and preparedness document, not an individualized dosing protocol. A breastfeeding adult is a conservative screening assumption until clarified. The user reports suitable cool/dark, child-locked storage and no fixed budget.

**Priority 1:** core supplies with broad utility or a serious operational role. **Priority 2:** useful additional coverage for common symptoms. **Priority 3:** narrower, duplicative, or condition/clinician-specific options. Rank reflects a qualitative judgment about usefulness, severity and safe self-care, not measured earthquake illness frequencies. A rescue medicine can become Priority 1 for a person who has a diagnosed need and a prescribed action plan.

**Confidence:** VERIFIED = actual guidance/label inspected in this session; ASSUMED = inventory quantity, rank or planning scenario; UNVERIFIED = unreadable or unconfirmed listing/specification; USER-CONFIRMED = the user's stated scope/preferences/existing supplies. DailyMed hosts manufacturer-submitted labels; its presence does not independently certify brand superiority or establish that a medicine is suitable for every person.

## Newborn and toddler boundaries

VERIFIED: A rectal temperature of **100.4°F / 38°C or higher in a baby 3 months or younger requires immediate pediatric assessment**, even if the baby otherwise looks well. Do not give a newborn a fever reducer first and assume a falling temperature means the cause is safe. Rectal readings are more reliable in young infants than ear readings; the pediatric team should demonstrate technique for the specific thermometer. Poor feeding, unusual lethargy, breathing difficulty, abnormal color or signs of dehydration also warrant urgent assessment. Sources: [aap_newborn_fever](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/fever/Pages/Fever-and-Your-Baby.aspx), [aap_dehydration](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/Pages/dehydration.aspx).

VERIFIED: Infants with diarrhea should continue their usual breast milk/formula feeds and receive clinical advice before ORS. Plain water is not routine newborn feeding; AAP introduces water around 6 months. Do not improvise diluted feeds, adult ORS portions, anti-diarrhea medicines, laxatives, sedating antihistamines, cough/cold medicine, antibiotics or eye drops for a newborn. Medicines in the family cache do not create a neonatal self-treatment plan. Sources: [niddk_diarrhea](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/diarrhea/treatment), [aap_water](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/nutrition/Pages/Choose-Water-for-Healthy-Hydration.aspx).

VERIFIED: The reviewed children's acetaminophen and ibuprofen labels begin their weight-based dosing rows at 24 lb. The toddler's current weight, discussed in chat, is below that row. AAP recommends weight-based guidance and mL-marked devices; obtain a written dose in mL for each exact concentration from the pediatrician rather than using the age row, splitting adult tablets or guessing. Ibuprofen is not approved for routine use under 6 months and needs additional caution with poor drinking or fluid loss. The liquid acetaminophen strength is 160 mg/5 mL; the selected children's ibuprofen strength is 100 mg/5 mL. These concentrations are purchase selectors, not doses. A separately marketed concentrated infant ibuprofen is not interchangeable volume-for-volume. Sources: the two pediatric labels in the table, [aap_acetaminophen](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/medication-safety/Pages/Acetaminophen-for-Fever-and-Pain.aspx), [aap_ibuprofen](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/medication-safety/Pages/Ibuprofen-for-Fever-and-Pain.aspx).

Do not routinely alternate acetaminophen and ibuprofen. ASSUMED operational choice: favor the simplest clinician-approved schedule with a written administration log to reduce duplicate doses by two caregivers. Ask the pharmacist for an oral syringe compatible with each product; some reviewed labels specify the supplied cup, so do not substitute a device contrary to the received instructions without pharmacy guidance.

Children's loratadine has label directions from age 2, but the actual child's plan should still be confirmed. Hydrocortisone's ordinary skin-itch directions start at age 2; diaper rash is explicitly excluded. Ketotifen eye drops direct under-3 children to a doctor. These facts do not establish universal eligibility for the toddler. FDA warns against cough/cold products in young children; keep this cache free of children's multi-symptom cough/cold medicines. Source: [fda_kids_cold](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/safe-use-over-counter-otc-medicines-children/use-caution-when-giving-cough-and-cold-products-kids).

## Burns and existing antiseptics

**The proposed burn-cream replacement is plain 100% white petrolatum in small tubes plus an appropriate nonstick sterile dressing**, if such dressings are not already in the first-aid bag. It is for a small superficial burn after cooling, not a treatment for large, deep, chemical or electrical burns or burns in a newborn.

VERIFIED: NHS recommends prompt cool running water for 20 minutes; AAD's first-degree-burn advice says cool water/compresses about 10 minutes or until pain subsides, then petroleum jelly and a nonstick sterile bandage. This difference is retained rather than claiming the sources give one identical protocol. The practical selection is **cool first**, aim for the NHS 20-minute cooling period for a small thermal burn while keeping the rest of the person warm, then follow the AAD petrolatum advice only if the injury is clearly superficial and suitable for home care. Do not put cream or oil on an unassessed acute burn, use ice, pop blisters, or substitute hydrogel/anaesthetic cream for cooling. Source: [nhs_burns](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/burns-and-scalds/), [aad_burns](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/injured-skin/burns/treat-minor-burns).

AAD directs an infant burn to emergency care; the cache should not support treating a newborn burn independently. Large/deep burns, chemical/electrical injury, facial/genital injury or uncertainty about severity need urgent assessment. Lidocaine/benzocaine burn products, silver sulfadiazine and silver/antibiotic creams are not default purchases for this household. The current sources support simple cooling and skin protection; specialised burn treatment belongs with a clinician.

For clean minor cuts, AAD favors cleaning, plain petroleum jelly and a dressing rather than routine topical antibiotics. Existing antiseptics, prescription antibiotics and wound-cleaning water have different roles. An ointment does not make an unclean, deep or infected wound safe.

## Antibiotics and nausea

**Topical antibiotics:** one Polysporin tube is an optional Priority 3 item. Its US label permits first aid for minor cuts/scrapes/burns, but AAD recommends against routine topical antibiotic use for clean minor cuts or superficial burns because skin irritation can be a drawback. Both are preserved: the product is labelled for that role, while routine use is not this plan's default. Bacitracin/polymyxin can still cause allergy; being neomycin-free does not make it allergy-proof. Neither wound ointment nor existing antiseptic is a substitute for assessment of spreading redness, pus, fever, bites, deep contamination or significant crush injury.

**Oral antibiotics:** an earthquake does not determine the bacterium, infection site, appropriate drug or treatment course. NHS says antibiotics treat selected bacterial infections, not viral colds/flu; NIDDK limits infectious-diarrhea antibiotic treatment to selected digestive infections. A child's prescribed formulation cannot simply be replaced by a portion of an adult pill. The exact pharmacist dispensing/storage plan controls; no assumed 90-day opened-liquid shelf life is used.

Discuss a small prescription reserve of ondansetron orally disintegrating tablets as Priority 2 if the clinician thinks it appropriate. ## Quantity model and water dependencies

All calculations are ASSUMED inventory sensitivities, not medication directions:

- The horizon is **90 days without dependable resupply**, not 90 days of continuous treatment.
- Illustration for adult tablet stock: four short illness/injury episodes across the household × 25 tablets consumed per episode = 100 tablets. A 200-caplet acetaminophen stock is 100% above that illustrative count; 100 ibuprofen tablets provide a separate option. This does not estimate illness probability, prescribe 25 tablets per episode, assume both drugs must be taken, or override short-use label limits. Substantial injury or prolonged illness exceeds this model.
- ORS: four hypothetical episodes × 5 L of prepared solution = 20 L; adding 50% gives **30 L** from 30 one-liter packets. Two sealed one-liter ready solutions add **2 L**, for **32 L total planned ORS capacity**. The five-liter episode assumption is not a prescribed adult or pediatric rehydration volume.
- Thirty liters of mixing water equals 30 ÷ 3.785411784 = **7.93 US gallons**. It comes out of the protected potable-water reserve; do not count the same water again as newly created capacity. ORS packets do not disinfect water. A litre is not a quart or 16-ounce bottle. Mix the entire packet into the exact volume printed on that packet; do not dry-split it or add extra sugar/salt.
- MiraLAX: 30 labelled 17 g doses = **510 g** of powder. Label mixing requires 4–8 fl oz per adult labelled dose; all 30 would use **0.94–1.88 US gallons** of safe liquid. This is a resource upper-bound for stock, not permission to use laxatives continuously for 30 days; the reviewed label limits unsupervised use to 7 days.
- Two 4-US-fl-oz bottles total approximately **236.6 mL**. Pediatric stock amounts do not establish treatment duration before the actual dose and use pattern are known.
- Two 16-US-fl-oz eyewash bottles total **946 mL / 0.25 US gallon**. They can start irrigation but do not supply a chemical-eye-injury protocol requiring continuous clean-water flushing for at least 15 minutes. Ensure additional accessible safe water. MedlinePlus directs immediate flushing and medical help; AAO distinguishes an embedded/punctured eye, which must not be rinsed or have an object removed.
- Two small topical tubes reduce one-package failure and allow one to remain unopened. No grams-per-day skin-treatment model is claimed. The same petrolatum supply overlaps chapped skin/diaper-barrier care, so zinc oxide is useful additional coverage rather than an independent survival requirement.

Receive a long remaining printed shelf life when practical, but do not assume a seller guarantees a particular expiry. Confirm every returned package size and ingredient before calculating owned stock. Use only safe water for mixing, wound care and eye irrigation; keep non-potable sanitation water separate.

## Storage and earthquake operations

VERIFIED: FDA advises following printed expiry and storage directions; an unexpired medicine can be compromised by heat or inappropriate storage. Many inspected oral medicines specify **20–25°C / 68–77°F**; other products have their own permitted ranges. A dark location alone does not solve heat. Keep the cache indoors, dry and child/pet-locked, in original labelled packaging rather than mixed pill bags or an anonymous bulk kit. Source: [fda_expiry](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/safe-disposal-medicines/dont-be-tempted-use-expired-medicines).

ASSUMED operating schedule: inspect every **6 months**, before travel, and immediately after significant heat, water, quake or package damage. Rotate by the received expiry, record purchase/expiry/opening dates, and obey any shorter after-opening or reconstitution limit. Check thermometer batteries; store matching spares per the device instructions. Do not freeze medicines or store them in a car/hot garage. Sterile single-use drops/eyewash must be discarded after opening as the specific label requires.

Maintain a paper, private medication card with each person's allergies, exact prescriptions, clinician-approved pediatric doses in mL, warnings, last administered time and clinician/pharmacy contacts. Keep a simple symptom/dose log during an outage so two caregivers cannot inadvertently repeat a medicine. Put essential supplies where seismic damage will not predictably trap the only copy, within child-access protections.

Escalation plan: neonatal fever/poor feeding, breathing difficulty, severe allergy, confusion, inability to retain fluid, marked dehydration, blood in stool/vomit, severe or worsening abdominal pain, spreading infection, serious burns and eye pain/vision change require care. Do not make taking a medicine a reason to delay that care. Plan an alternate route/site from the existing navigation work and carry paper information if phones are down. US Poison Help: **1-800-222-1222**. Collapse, seizure or breathing difficulty: **911**. Sources: [poison_help](https://poisonhelp.org/), [medline_anaphylaxis](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000844.htm).

For simple loose eye grit, do not rub; flush appropriately. For a cut/puncture or embedded object, protect the eye without pressure and get urgent care. Avoid redness-whitening drops, unsupervised steroid/antibiotic eye drops and skin antiseptics in the eye. Refresh Plus and Zaditor have comfort/allergy roles, not trauma treatment roles.

## Method, alternatives and limitations

M0: the user's diagnostic defined the patient groups, horizon, symptom scope, lack of fixed budget and suitable storage. M1: inspected federal/pediatric/dermatology and drug-label guidance. M2: screened single-ingredient exact formulations, child-use limits, lactation limitations, skin/eye roles and representative manufacturer identities. M3: normalized stock, ranked a core against optional expansion, and retained contrary evidence.

Alternatives rejected or demoted:

- Buying an all-in-one medicine kit: package size does not establish age suitability, correct strengths or a coherent dosing plan.
- Treating ORS as interchangeable with any sports/electrolyte powder: choose an actual oral rehydration formulation and follow its exact mixing instructions. Brand-specific WHO/NSF claims from TRIORAL were not independently certified in this batch.
- Using antibiotic or numbing burn creams as the default: cooling and simple protection have direct support for a superficial burn; specialized therapy requires assessment.
- Stocking multiple NSAIDs, sedating antihistamines, PM tablets and multi-symptom cold medicines: duplication and impairment add avoidable mistakes during evacuation/childcare. Plain loratadine was selected instead of routine diphenhydramine. Do not combine antihistamines without instructions.
- Using daily probiotics as the primary diarrhea reserve: NIDDK states evidence is not considered supportive by many doctors/societies; hydration is the core.
- Buying a large antibiotic bundle for unspecified future infections: diagnosis/indication and patient-specific instructions remain unsolved.
- Self-treating every eye symptom as a minor irritation: trauma, infection, chemical injury and dryness require different responses.

Access limitations are explicit. Direct Amazon searches returned no readable product inventory/pricing in this session.
