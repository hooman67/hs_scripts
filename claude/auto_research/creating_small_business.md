# Creating a Small Business: Start, Buy, or Franchise?

**Decision report for Hooman Shariati**  
**Research date:** 2026-09-10  
**Objective:** build a second income stream producing **$5,000–$30,000/month in cash profit**, sustainable at **10–20 owner hours/week**, with **$25,000–$150,000 of capital at risk**, preferably without a personal guarantee.

## Executive decision

The best next move is to **start a narrow, productized field-service operations business**, not a generic consultancy and not a software startup. Sell a fixed-price “revenue leakage and workflow teardown,” implement one automation, then convert the same workflow into a managed monthly service. This is the only path in the current budget that can plausibly produce first revenue in 2–8 weeks and reach $5,000/month profit without first buying a full-time job.

In parallel—but as a search, not a second operating business—look off-market for a **manager-light recurring service company**: commercial landscape maintenance first, structural pest control second, commercial janitorial third. Buy only if the existing owner truly works no more than 20 hours/week, a crew lead or licensed qualifier will remain, and normalized cash flow after financing still exceeds $60,000/year. Good versions of this deal are scarce and usually cost more than $150,000; the acquisition path therefore requires unusually favorable seller financing secured only by the acquired business.

Franchising ranks third. The brand and playbook can reduce setup ambiguity, but it does not buy customers, employees, or owner independence. The one concept worth diligencing is **Dryer Vent Wizard's executive model**; its advertised investment range is $84,900–$163,400, but profitability and owner hours remain unverified until the current FDD and franchisee calls prove them. Most other manager-style home-service franchises either start at or above the capital ceiling or need full-time owner selling during ramp.

The hard truth: **$5,000/month is plausible; $30,000/month at 10–20 hours/week is not a reasonable base case for any $25,000–$150,000 small-business entry.** $30,000/month generally requires a multi-crew platform, unusually strong productized-service sales, more capital, or several years of compounding.

### Confidence notation

- **VERIFIED** — supported by a primary source or current marketplace data.
- **ASSUMED** — planning estimate; must be tested before capital is committed.
- **UNVERIFIED** — seller/franchisor/vendor claim or unavailable evidence.

All economics below are **monthly pre-tax cash profit after ordinary operating expenses**. For a business that needs more than 20 owner hours/week, I subtract replacement labor or a manager before calling the result “profit.” This avoids treating the owner's full-time wage as investment return.

Current transaction evidence makes the acquisition constraint visible. BizBuySell's Q2 2026 report says the median sold business cost **$349,250**, median cash flow was **$155,921**, and the average cash-flow multiple was **2.7×**. Service businesses were 40% of transactions and their median sale price was **$350,000**. It also reports that recurring-revenue service companies remain highly sought after. [VERIFIED; seller/broker-reported transaction dataset] [S1]

At a 2.7× market multiple, $150,000 buys only about **$55,600/year ($4,630/month) of SDE**. SDE includes one owner's labor; it is not manager-normalized profit. Therefore a normal all-cash acquisition at the budget ceiling misses the $5,000/month target even before replacing the seller. [VERIFIED arithmetic on S1]

Seller financing does not automatically solve this. BizBuySell reports **90% of buyers expect seller financing, but only 29% of sellers plan to offer it**. [VERIFIED, S1] A $450,000 business at 2.5× SDE implies $180,000 SDE. With $150,000 down and a hypothetical $300,000 seller note at 8% over seven years, debt service is about $56,100/year. It can leave roughly $10,300/month only if the seller already works no more than 20 hours/week; adding a $90,000 replacement operator cuts cash to about $2,800/month. [ASSUMED financing terms; VERIFIED amortization arithmetic]

That is the governing distinction:

> Buy a business where a foreman already runs delivery and the seller performs only the 10–20 hours Hooman is willing to inherit. Do not buy SDE that disappears when the seller stops working.

### #1 — Productized field-service revenue recovery and AI operations

**The actual product:** sell one narrow operating outcome to HVAC, plumbing, roofing, pest, or landscape companies with 20–200 staff:

1. a $2,500–$4,000, two-week workflow teardown;
2. one deployed automation, such as missed-call recovery, estimate follow-up, scheduling exception handling, invoice chasing, or technician-note QA;
3. a $1,000–$2,500/month managed outcome service with a human review layer.

This is a small service business, not “build another AI startup.” Customers buy recovered jobs, faster estimates, or fewer scheduling mistakes. The software stack remains replaceable; the defensible assets become the vertical workflow library, before/after outcome data, integrations, references, and distribution relationships. [ASSUMED strategy; based on completed project research]

**Why it fits Hooman:** his technical credibility and systems skill reduce delivery risk. More importantly, paid service work creates the customer access that his prior software ideas lacked. He should not promise novel models or Adobe-derived technology. Employment/IP/conflict review remains Gate 0; use only personal accounts, hardware, code, and time. [VERIFIED that this is a material project-specific constraint; legal interpretation requires counsel]

**Economics, red-teamed:**

| Case | Operating shape | Monthly revenue | Cash profit | Hooman hours |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Bear | One $3K teardown every two months + one $1K retainer | $2.5K | **$1K–$2K** | 8–15 |
| Base | One $4K teardown/month + five $1.5K retainers | $11.5K | **$6K–$9K** | 15–20 after templates and contractor support |
| Bull | Two $5K projects/month + eight $2K retainers | $26K | **$13K–$17K** | ≤20 only with a capable delivery contractor |

All three cases are **ASSUMED**. The base requires approximately 25%–40% delivery/tool/contractor cost and a repeatable scope. If every engagement remains bespoke, hours expand and the model fails.

**Fastest validation:** sell before building. Contact 40–50 warm owners and referral nodes, hold ten discovery calls, and close two paid teardowns. Use existing CRM and automation tools; do not write a platform. The observed repeated workflow—not Hooman's preferred technology—decides the managed service.

**Kill:** no two paid teardowns within eight weeks; no repeated pain across the first four engagements; delivery remains above 20 hours/client after the second implementation; or clients will not retain at $1,000+/month.

### #2 — Acquire a manager-light recurring local service platform

**Buy box, in order:**

1. commercial/residential landscape **maintenance** with a crew foreman (not design-build);
2. structural pest-control recurring accounts with a continuing licensed Operator/qualifying manager;
3. commercial janitorial with a crew lead and diversified contracts;
4. pool service only if it is already a multi-tech company, not a seller's personal route.

The attractive asset is not a truck or equipment. It is a dense, recurring customer book plus a delivery lead who stays after closing. Route density, renewal data, reviews, and a reliable crew are the moat. Hooman can add dispatch, churn prediction, quote follow-up, and technician QA, but those improvements do not rescue a bad purchase price.

California licensing is a real gate. CSLB says anyone contracting for work that requires a permit, uses employee labor, or totals $1,000 or more in labor and materials must hold the appropriate contractor license. [VERIFIED, S4] The Structural Pest Control Board says only a licensed Operator can own/operate their own pest-control company or act as qualifying manager for another owner. [VERIFIED, S5] Thus a nontechnical buyer can own the entity, but continuity of the qualifying person must be a closing condition; exact structure requires California counsel and board confirmation.

**Required deal shape:**

- purchase price ≤$450K–$500K;
- Hooman cash ≤$150K; balance is a long-duration seller note, secured only by acquired assets, with no personal guarantee [ASSUMED/negotiated];
- verified SDE ≥$180K;
- seller's actual work ≤20 hr/wk, supported by calendars, payroll, CRM activity, and customer calls;
- crew lead/qualifier signs an employment and retention agreement before closing;
- ≥60% recurring revenue; no customer >15%; three-year cohort retention measurable;
- normalized post-debt cash ≥$60K/year in a 15% revenue-decline stress case.

**Economics, red-teamed:**

| Case | Assumptions | Monthly cash after replacement labor and seller-note debt |
|---|---|---:|
| Bear | $120K SDE, seller actually full-time, $90K replacement operator, ~$56K debt service | **negative** |
| Base | $180K SDE, seller truly 10–20 hr/wk and Hooman assumes those hours, ~$56K debt service | **~$10.3K** |
| Bull | $250K SDE, foreman runs delivery, same debt service | **~$16.2K** |

These are **ASSUMED illustrations**, not deal forecasts. The base case is deliberately a hard buy box because ordinary inventory does not fit. Current market demand makes it difficult: BizBuySell reports quality, recurring, manager-backed businesses command buyer competition, while median time on market for service transactions was 155 days in Q2 2026. [VERIFIED, S1]

**Fastest validation:** do not start with marketplace browsing alone. Send 75 concise letters/emails to 10–30-year-old local firms, call 20 owners, and ask brokers specifically for “seller 20 hours or less, foreman in place, maintenance revenue, $150K down, seller-financed.” A willing retiring owner with clean books matters more than category perfection.

**Kill:** seller labor cannot be reconstructed; tax returns do not reconcile; owner-independent cash after debt is below $60K; seller note requires a personal guarantee or home collateral; qualifying manager will not stay; top customer >15%; recurring revenue below 60%; or employee classification/payroll/insurance is not clean.

### #3 — Commercial janitorial contract business

This can be started with modest equipment or acquired as a customer book. Target professional offices, dental groups, small industrial facilities, and multi-site property managers—not one-off residential cleaning. Win a narrow service-level promise, use inspection photos and issue tracking, and assign a working crew lead. The software layer improves QA and customer reporting; it is not the moat.

California adds meaningful compliance. Janitorial employers must register annually with the Labor Commissioner's Office, pay a $500 fee, keep required records, and provide prescribed sexual-violence/harassment-prevention training. [VERIFIED, S6] California's ABC test also makes casual “1099 cleaner” assumptions dangerous; workers are employees unless all parts of the test are satisfied. [VERIFIED, S7]

### #5 — Buy a dense pool route, then hire delivery

A pool route produces immediate recurring billing and can be bought in small pieces. One current route broker says routes commonly sell for **10–12× monthly service billing**, that a 50-pool solo route produces about **$9,250/month service revenue** at $185/pool, and that employee-serviced margins commonly fall to **20%–40%**. It also explicitly describes a 50-pool route as 30–35 field hours/week. These are broker/vendor claims, not audited industry statistics. [UNVERIFIED, S9]

That distinction kills the passive story. A roughly $100K–$120K route may produce excellent owner-operator income, but only about $2K–$4K/month at the vendor's employee-margin claim. Repairs can add upside, but may require technical expertise and California contractor licensing. A route is best treated as a hands-on apprenticeship or first roll-up unit, not a finished 10-hour/week income stream.

**Bear/base/bull cash profit:** approximately **$1.5K / $3K / $5K per month** on a $100K–$150K acquired route with hired delivery [ASSUMED from the vendor ranges]. More than $5K usually requires owner field labor, a larger route beyond the cash ceiling, or repair/install revenue.

**Kill:** fewer than 40 dense accounts; account churn above 15% during transfer guarantee; seller will not provide billing history and ride-along; drive time exceeds 25% of technician hours; or the model needs unlicensed repair work.

## 3. The important economics: the manager test

The income target must always be stated two ways:

1. **Owner-operator cash:** what remains while Hooman performs the seller's job.
2. **Owner-independent cash:** what remains after paying for every hour above his 10–20-hour allowance.

For Bay Area planning, use **$90K–$130K/year fully loaded** for a competent small-service general manager until actual candidates and payroll quotes replace the assumption. [ASSUMED] The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes local occupation-and-wage data that should anchor role-specific normalization, but “general and operations manager” averages include much larger firms and should not be copied mechanically into a small-business model. [VERIFIED source availability, S12]

Examples:

- A listing with $180K SDE and a 50-hour seller is **not** a $180K investment return. After a $110K manager, it is about $70K before acquisition financing.
- A listing with $180K SDE and a 15-hour seller may genuinely fit: Hooman can inherit those hours, preserve most SDE, and automate selectively.
- A $100K pool route with $8K–$10K monthly billing is not “passive.” The owner-operated margin and the hired-technician margin are different businesses.
- A franchise's gross-sales average is not profit. Only Item 19, expense reconstruction, and current/former franchisee calls can support an earnings case. [VERIFIED process, S11]

For every opportunity, reconstruct a weekly calendar from source evidence: sales calls, estimates, dispatch, field production, quality control, customer saves, payroll, hiring, bookkeeping, after-hours incidents, and owner-only technical work. If any recurring task lacks a named employee and payroll cost, assume the seller performs it.

## 4. Fast validation by path

### Start from scratch

The right validation unit is a **paid job**, not a website or LLC.

1. Choose one offer and one buyer. Recommended: missed-call/estimate-follow-up recovery for 20–200-person home-service operators.
2. Use a one-page scope and fixed price. Contact 40–50 warm prospects/referral nodes plus 25 carefully selected cold prospects.
3. Ask for a paid $2.5K–$4K teardown; do not give away a custom implementation as “discovery.”
4. Deliver with existing tools and a contractor only where necessary.
5. Track pain, before/after dollars, implementation hours, recurring work, and who owns the needed data/API.

**Spend ceiling before signal:** $5,000. [ASSUMED]  
**Signal:** two paid teardowns in eight weeks and at least two clients asking for the same ongoing workflow.  
**Then:** package the repeated workflow as a managed service; software comes later, if ever.

### Acquire

The right validation unit is a **reconciled earnings package**, not a listing teaser.

1. Build a one-page buy box and proof-of-funds letter.
2. Contact local business brokers, CPAs, suppliers, and 75 owners directly; public listings are a secondary feed.
3. Require three years of tax returns, monthly P&L, bank statements, payroll detail, customer cohorts, owner calendar, employee roster, licenses, claims, and contracts.
4. Reconcile revenue independently; normalize every family-member wage and owner task.
5. Submit an LOI only when the 15%-revenue-down case leaves $60K/year after debt and necessary replacement labor.

**Spend ceiling before signed LOI:** $3,000 for targeted legal/accounting advice. [ASSUMED]  
**Signal:** two businesses pass financial reconciliation and one seller accepts the capped-risk structure.  
**Then:** quality of earnings, legal/licensing review, customer calls, employee retention agreements, and escrow.

### Franchise

The right validation unit is **the current FDD plus non-curated franchisee evidence**, not a discovery-day presentation.

1. Request current FDDs from Dryer Vent Wizard and one independent-service comparison.
2. Read Items 3, 5–7, 8, 11–12, 17, 19, 20, and 21.
3. Call at least eight current owners, including newer and median performers, plus five owners who left or transferred.
4. Rebuild a Bay Area P&L with local wages, insurance, fuel, ad spend, royalty, call-center/technology fees, vehicle replacement, and a field technician.
5. Ask each owner for actual weekly hours in months 1–6 and after year 1.

California maintains a franchise filing search, useful for retrieving or confirming registered offerings. [VERIFIED, S13] The FTC's 14-day rule is a minimum review period, not a reason to rush. [VERIFIED, S11]

**Spend before proof:** $0 beyond professional review of an actual FDD.  
**Signal:** median—not top-quartile—mature units support at least $8K/month after a technician/manager and all fees.  
**Then:** territory-level demand test and lawyer review.

## 5. Recommended 30/90-day sequence

### Days 1–7: clear gates and prepare both experiments

- Review Adobe employment, invention-assignment, outside-work, confidentiality, and conflict rules with an employment attorney. Keep all work on personal time, hardware, accounts, repositories, and datasets. This is a launch gate for the AI-service path, not optional hygiene.
- Finalize one productized offer: **“Revenue Leakage Teardown + One Live Automation” at $2,500–$4,000.**
- Create the acquisition buy box above and a 20-question owner-interview script.
- Request the Dryer Vent Wizard FDD, solely as a benchmark; pay nothing.

### Days 8–30: run three cheap tests, operate none at full scale

- **Start test:** 40–50 warm/referral contacts, 25 cold targets, ten calls, goal of two paid teardowns.
- **Buy test:** 75 owner letters/emails plus broker/CPA outreach, goal of five owner conversations and two complete financial packages.
- **Franchise test:** read the FDD and call 8 current + 5 former franchisees. Stop immediately if Item 19 is absent or incomparable.

At day 30, choose **one operating lane**. The acquisition search may continue quietly, but do not simultaneously launch an AI service, field-service crew, and franchise.

### Days 31–60: prove the unit

If the productized service wins, deliver two engagements, measure hard ROI, and sell the first two retainers. Keep a repeatable-pain log. If acquisition wins, perform reconciliation and an owner-hours audit before LOI. If the dryer-vent independent test wins, fulfill ten paid jobs through a qualified insured provider before buying equipment.

### Days 61–90: prove repeatability and delegation

- Productized service: reach 3–4 completed engagements, three retainers, and document delivery so a contractor can own at least 50%.
- Acquisition: issue at most one LOI that satisfies the stressed cash-flow and no-personal-guarantee rules.
- Franchise: proceed only if the FDD and franchisee calls clear every threshold; otherwise close the file.

**Day-90 default decision:** continue the productized service if it has two paid projects plus two recurring customers. Continue acquisition search if there are real owner conversations but no safe deal. Do not lower the buy box to force a transaction.

## 6. Portfolio-level kill criteria

Kill or pause any candidate when one of these is true:

1. **Time:** Hooman is still above 20 hours/week after the 90-day learning period and delegation would push profit below $5K/month.
2. **Income:** the honest base case cannot reach $60K/year pre-tax cash profit within 12 months for an acquisition or 18 months for a startup.
3. **Downside:** financing requires a personal guarantee, home lien, or uncapped capital call.
4. **Owner dependence:** more than 25% of sales, delivery, or technical work relies on the seller with no trained replacement.
5. **Concentration:** one customer exceeds 15% or the top five exceed 40%, absent unusually strong transferable contracts.
6. **Labor fiction:** the model needs core workers to be treated as 1099 contractors despite California's ABC test. [S7]
7. **License/key-person:** a required qualifier will not sign through transition, or the business depends on unlicensed work.
8. **Weak retention:** recurring-service annual churn above 20% or route-transfer loss above 15%.
9. **Franchise evidence:** earnings claims exist outside Item 19, median economics are unavailable, or current/former franchisees contradict the sales presentation.
10. **Tech vanity:** automation saves less than one full-time role or measurable lost revenue, yet the acquisition price assumes a large “AI upside.”

## 7. What to do—and what not to do

**Do now:** run the 30-day productized-service sales test while beginning a low-intensity off-market search for a manager-light maintenance/pest/janitorial company. This gives the fastest path to first revenue and preserves the chance to buy a durable customer base later.

**Do not do now:** build software before paid service evidence; buy a lone route and call it passive; buy a franchise on gross-sales claims; open a restaurant/retail location; or accept a personal guarantee to make an otherwise unaffordable acquisition appear feasible.

The most likely successful sequence is:

> **Start a cash-light service → discover a repeatable operating pain → build recurring managed revenue → acquire a small customer book or competitor once cash flow and distribution exist.**

This is better than choosing “start” or “buy” forever. It uses Hooman's strongest advantage immediately, caps the first loss at a few thousand dollars, and lets a later acquisition buy distribution from a position of operating knowledge.
