# Finding & Contacting SMB Clients — A Solo Operator's Playbook

**For:** Hooman Shariati — Adobe Foundry engineering manager (builds production AI for Coca-Cola, Home Depot, Ford, Tesco, Humain), launching a **solo AI-consulting practice for small-and-medium businesses**, initially **part-time while employed at Adobe**.

**The core problem this file solves:** At Adobe, deals come from Adobe's sales org and brand. Solo, you have **neither a pipeline nor a brand**. This is a playbook to manufacture both — fast, cheaply, and in the ~10 hrs/week a part-timer actually has.

**Last updated:** 2026-08-27 by GTM/sales-strategy agent.

---

## 0. The one-paragraph thesis

You do **not** need a marketing engine. You need **1–3 paying clients**. At an SMB consulting price point (say $5–15K per project or $3–8K/mo retainer), three clients is a meaningful second income and a full part-time load. That changes everything about strategy: **you optimize for conversion, not reach.** The entire ranked order of channels below flows from that single fact — warm paths convert 10–50x better than cold, so you exhaust them *first* and only turn on cold volume to fill gaps.

**Channel priority (highest expected conversion → lowest):**
1. Warm network & referrals (do this for weeks 1–4, keep forever)
2. Local / community / vertical owner-communities (weeks 2–8)
3. Content on LinkedIn (start week 1, compounds over months)
4. Direct cold outbound — email + LinkedIn (weeks 4+, once positioning is proven)
5. Freelance marketplaces (optional, one specific play only)

## 1. Warm / network-first paths (do this FIRST — highest conversion)

### Why warm dominates the math
- **88% of people place their highest trust in a brand recommended by a friend or family member**; ~90% are more likely to trust a recommended brand. Word-of-mouth is "directly responsible for ~90% of purchases" and drives ~5x more sales than paid ads. [VERIFIED — Semrush word-of-mouth stats compilation, fetched 2026-08-27]
- Practical translation for you: a warm intro to an SMB owner converts to a first call at a rate that is *categorically* higher than cold email's ~3–8% reply rate (see §2). Assume a warm intro → discovery-call rate of **40–70%**, and warm-lead → close of **20–40%**. [ASSUMED — extrapolated from the trust data above + standard B2B services experience; no single source measured this exact funnel.]

A solo operator with a good network can often land the first 1–2 clients **entirely warm**, before ever sending a cold email. That is the goal for the first 30 days.

### How to mine a warm list (mechanics — do this in week 1)
Build a single spreadsheet, `warm_list.csv`, with columns: `Name | Relationship | Company | Role | Do they own/run an SMB or know owners? | Warmth (1-5) | Ask type | Status | Last touch`.

Populate it from these sources, in order:

1. **LinkedIn 1st-degree connections export.** Settings → Data Privacy → Get a copy of your data → Connections. This is the single richest seed. Filter for anyone who is a **founder/owner/president/CEO of a company under ~200 people**, or who plausibly *knows* such people (agency owners, accountants, lawyers, fractional CFOs, wealth managers — these are referral super-nodes). [VERIFIED — LinkedIn's native connections export.]
2. **Ex-colleagues who left to start or join startups.** Adobe alumni are everywhere and many now run or work at SMBs. They already know you're technically credible — that's the whole game.
3. **"Founder friends" and your EE/mech-engineer network.** The CLAUDE.md notes you have mechanical-engineer friends and a founder network. Founders know other founders; that's your referral spine.
4. **Adjacent professionals who sell to SMBs.** Your accountant, your lawyer, your insurance broker, your realtor, your kids'-school parents who own businesses. These people **talk to dozens of SMB owners a week** and are natural referral engines once they understand what you do.
5. **Local alumni & professional groups** you already belong to (university alumni chapter, any professional Slack/Discord).

### The two asks (never ask "do you know anyone who needs AI?")
That question gets a polite "I'll keep an eye out" and nothing happens — it's too vague to act on. Use these instead:

- **The specific-referral ask (to warm nodes):**
  > "I'm helping small businesses use AI to cut a specific cost — like automating quoting, customer support triage, or document processing. Do you know **one** owner who's drowning in manual work or curious about AI? Even a name and a one-line intro helps enormously."
  (Naming a concrete, imaginable pain and asking for *one* name makes it actionable.)

- **The advice ask (to owners themselves — the Trojan horse):**
  > "I'm starting something on the side and I trust your judgment. Can I get 20 minutes to show you what I'm building and get your honest take? No pitch — I genuinely want a reality check from someone who runs a business."
  This "advice call" converts to a client more often than a sales call, because it's disarming and half of them will self-identify a problem you can solve on the call.

### Referral hygiene
- Make the intro **frictionless**: write the forwardable blurb *for* the connector so all they do is hit forward. ("Here's a 3-line note you can paste to anyone who comes to mind.")
- **Close the loop**: always report back to the referrer what happened. It triggers more referrals and it's basic decency.
- **Ask every happy client for a referral** at the moment of a visible win — this is how a solo practice compounds without a marketing budget. Referral is the *cheapest customer-acquisition channel* and referred customers carry higher lifetime value. [VERIFIED — Semrush word-of-mouth data, loyalty "worth ten times a single purchase."]

## 2. Direct outbound that works for SMB (cold email + LinkedIn)

Turn this on **after** warm paths are running and *after* you have at least one proof point (a case study, even a free/discounted first project). Cold outbound with no proof and no niche converts near zero.

### The honest conversion math (memorize this)
- **B2B cold email average reply rate ≈ 3.4%** across all outbound; some sources put a broader "average" at ~8.5%. Tiering: **1–5% = average, 5–10% = good, 10%+ = excellent.** [VERIFIED — lemlist cold-email benchmark (3.43% avg; 18% with advanced personalization) and Klenty (8.5% avg, 23.9% open), fetched 2026-08-27.]
- **Personalization is the single biggest lever:** advanced personalization pushes reply rates to **~18%**. [VERIFIED — lemlist.]
- **Small lists crush big lists:** lists **under 50 recipients averaged 5.8% reply vs 2.1% for lists over 1,000.** [VERIFIED — lemlist.] This is decisive for you: **go narrow and hand-personalized, not broad and templated.**
- **Sequences win:** most replies come within the first **4–5 follow-ups**; 4–7 touches is best practice. A one-shot email is a wasted email. [VERIFIED — lemlist/Klenty.]
- **Multichannel (email + LinkedIn + call) beats single-channel.** [VERIFIED — Klenty.]
- **LinkedIn Sales Navigator:** +7% higher win rate, +18% pipeline for users. [VERIFIED — demandsage LinkedIn stats compilation.] Treat LinkedIn connection acceptance for a warm-ish, personalized request as **~30–40%** and reply-after-connect meaningfully higher than cold email. [ASSUMED — widely cited industry range; specific benchmark pages were unreachable at fetch time.]

### What the math means in units of work
To land clients purely cold, work backward:
- Target funnel (realistic, personalized, narrow list): **~10% reply → ~30% of replies become a call → ~25% of calls close.**
- So: **100 well-researched, personalized prospects → ~10 replies → ~3 calls → ~1 client.** [ASSUMED — composed from the verified reply/personalization data above; call-and-close rates are experience-based estimates. Mark as a hypothesis to re-measure with your own numbers.]
- **Implication:** budget ~100–150 *genuinely personalized* touches per client. That is 2–3 hours/week of list-building + sending, sustainable part-time. Do **not** blast 2,000 generic emails — the data says that's *worse* (2.1% vs 5.8%) *and* it burns your domain.

### List-building stack (use your own AI skills — this is your unfair advantage)
- **Apollo.io** — cheapest all-in-one: B2B contact database + email finder + sequencer. Best starting point for SMB targeting (filter by industry, headcount 10–200, geography, tech stack). [ASSUMED — well-known tool positioning.]
- **Clay** — the power tool: pulls from many data sources and lets you run **LLM enrichment per row** (e.g., "read this company's website and write one sentence on how AI could cut their ops cost"). This is where *you* out-execute every other consultant: you can script genuine per-prospect personalization at scale using the exact skills you'd be selling. That's both efficient *and* a live demo of your capability. [ASSUMED — Clay's documented use case.]
- **LinkedIn Sales Navigator** — best for building the targeted list of *owners* by title + company size + geography, and for warm-ish social outreach. [VERIFIED — the +win-rate/+pipeline stat above.]
- **Deliverability hygiene (non-negotiable):** send from a **separate domain** (not your primary), warm it up for 2–3 weeks before scaling, keep daily volume low (20–40/day from a new domain), verify emails before sending. [VERIFIED — Klenty lists warmup + low volume as high-performer traits.] This protects your reputation and your real email.

### Cold-email templates (tailored to the enterprise-credential angle)

**Template A — cold email, initial touch (short, specific, credibility-forward):**
> **Subject:** the AI thing [Competitor/peer] is probably already testing
>
> Hi [First name],
>
> I build production AI systems at Adobe for very large companies — the kind of automation most [industry, e.g. "specialty distributors"] assume is out of reach. It isn't anymore.
>
> I noticed [one specific, researched detail — "you still take quote requests by phone/email," "your team manually processes X"]. That's exactly the kind of repetitive work I can now automate for a business your size in a few weeks.
>
> Worth a 15-minute call to see if there's a quick win? If not, no worries.
>
> [Name] · [link to one case study / short demo]

**Template B — follow-up #2 (value-add, no ask):**
> Hi [First name] — quick follow-up. I recorded a 2-minute Loom showing how I'd automate [their specific task] for a company like yours: [link]. Even if we never work together, it might spark an idea for your team. Happy to talk if useful.

**Template C — LinkedIn connection note (< 300 chars):**
> Hi [First name] — I build AI for large enterprises at Adobe and I'm now helping smaller [industry] businesses use the same tech. Would love to connect and share what's actually working (and what's hype). No pitch.

**Template D — LinkedIn message after they accept:**
> Thanks for connecting, [First name]. Genuinely curious: what's the most repetitive, time-sucking task your team does that you wish would just… handle itself? I've been automating exactly that kind of thing and might have a quick idea for you.

**Why these work:** they lead with the *transferable* credential (§6), name a *specific* observed pain, ask for tiny commitment, and give value before asking. The Loom/demo in Template B is your differentiator — see §4.

## 3. Local / in-person & community channels

SMB owners are **directly reachable** (your thesis is correct) and disproportionately live in a handful of high-signal places. Ranked by signal-for-effort:

### Highest signal
- **Vertical Facebook Groups & niche owner communities.** This project's own hard-won finding: *"Facebook groups are the highest-signal validation channel for B2B — trade shop owners (laser/CNC/welding) are active there."* [VERIFIED — auto_research/CLAUDE.md §6 learnings.] The same is true across trades, e-commerce, agencies, restaurants, med/dental practices, real estate. **Pick 2–3 groups for one vertical you'll specialize in, and become a genuinely helpful regular** (answer questions, never spam). One well-known helpful presence in a 20K-owner group is worth more than 1,000 cold emails.
- **BNI (Business Network International) and similar structured referral groups.** These exist *specifically* to trade referrals; one seat per profession per chapter. As "the AI/automation person," you'd be the only one, and members are *obligated* to pass referrals. High signal but requires weekly commitment and a ~$1K/yr-ish fee — evaluate against your part-time hours. [ASSUMED — BNI's documented model.]
- **Local industry / trade meetups (Meetup.com, Eventbrite) for a specific vertical**, not generic "startup" or "tech" meetups (those are peers, not buyers). A restaurant-owner association meetup > a "founders coffee."

### Medium signal
- **Chambers of Commerce.** Broad, mixed quality, lots of solo networkers. Useful for local credibility and a handful of intros; not a volume channel. Go to a couple, don't over-invest.
- **Industry-specific Slack/Discord communities** (e.g., agency-owner Slacks, e-commerce Discords, trade forums). Same rule as Facebook: contribute value, don't pitch.
- **Local business associations / rotary / merchant associations** — good for the "trusted local expert" positioning, slower burn.

### The community rule
Every community channel follows one rule: **give value publicly for weeks before you ever mention you're for hire.** Post a useful teardown ("here's how a shop like ours could auto-generate quotes"), answer questions, and let inbound DMs come to you. Pitching on day one gets you muted; being the helpful expert gets you hired. This mirrors the content strategy in §4.

## 4. Inbound / content that compounds

Content is the flywheel that makes cold outreach unnecessary over time. As a solo part-timer you can't produce volume, so produce **proof**, not think-pieces.

### What actually converts SMB owners (in order)
1. **Concrete before/after case studies with a dollar figure.** "I automated [Company X]'s quote process — from 45 min/quote to 3 min, ~$40K/yr of labor back." SMB owners buy ROI, not technology.
2. **"I automated X for a business like yours" short demos.** A 2–4 minute Loom/video walking through a real automation is the single highest-converting artifact for a technical founder — it *proves* capability instead of claiming it. Attach these to cold emails (Template B) and post them.
3. **ROI teardowns / "here's the math" posts.** Show the arithmetic of an automation. Owners screenshot and share these.
4. **"Hype vs reality" educational content** — you have the credibility to say what AI *can't* do yet, which builds more trust than hype.

### Where to post (as a solo, be ruthless — pick one primary)
- **LinkedIn is the primary channel.** It's where B2B buyers are, its cost-per-lead is ~28% lower than Google Ads, and it rewards consistent posting. [VERIFIED — demandsage LinkedIn stats.] Post 2–3x/week: case studies, demos, teardowns. Your Adobe-engineer profile *is* the credibility.
- **Secondary:** repurpose the same content into the vertical Facebook groups / Slacks from §3, and as short videos.
- **Ignore for now:** a blog/SEO (too slow), Twitter/X (wrong audience for SMB owners), TikTok (unless your vertical genuinely lives there).

### Your unfair content advantage: image/video generation
You build **generative image/video models (Firefly) for a living.** Use it:
- Turn dry case studies into **short, polished explainer videos / animated before-afters** that look far better than a typical consultant's talking-head Loom. Standing out visually on LinkedIn is a real edge and it *demonstrates the exact skill you sell*.
- Generate custom diagrams/thumbnails for each post so your content is instantly recognizable in-feed.
- This is meta-proof: "the person selling AI automation obviously uses AI to make their own content look this good" is a strong implicit pitch.

**Content is compounding but slow — treat it as the long game running in parallel with warm+outbound, not a substitute for them in the first 90 days.**

## 5. Freelance marketplaces — honest assessment

**Short answer: mostly a race to the bottom for what you're selling, with one narrow exception. Do not build your business here, but one platform is worth a low-effort experiment.**

- **Upwork / Fiverr:** buyer intent skews toward *cheapest bidder*; you compete with global low-cost freelancers and get commoditized. Fees eat a chunk, and the positioning actively *undercuts* your enterprise-credential premium. **Skip as a primary channel.** [ASSUMED — well-documented marketplace dynamics; Upwork pages were unreachable at fetch time.] Narrow exception: if you want *one* quick paid proof-of-concept to generate your first case study, a single well-chosen Upwork gig can do that — treat it as R&D, not a business line.
- **Toptal:** genuinely premium — **top ~3% acceptance, 98% trial-to-hire**, positioned explicitly as "the world's top talent," serves developers/consultants/PMs. [VERIFIED — Toptal site, fetched 2026-08-27.] If accepted, it delivers *vetted, higher-budget* clients without you doing the sales — which is exactly your gap. **Worth applying to**; passing their screen is itself a credibility badge. Downside: you're still a resource in their marketplace, and they take a margin.
- **Contra / others:** "commission-free for freelancers" positioning, lighter vetting than Toptal; lower volume. Low downside to a profile but don't expect it to be a pipeline. [ASSUMED — Contra's stated model; page unreachable at fetch time.]

**Positioning to avoid commoditization anywhere you do appear:** never list "AI developer, $X/hr." List **outcomes and the enterprise pedigree** — "I build the production AI systems Fortune 500s run; I now do fixed-scope automation projects for SMBs." Price by **project/outcome, not hourly**, so you're not benchmarked against $15/hr freelancers.

**Verdict:** Apply to **Toptal** (free option value + a badge). Optionally do **one** Upwork gig purely to manufacture a first case study. Everything else, skip — your warm network and enterprise brand are worth more than any marketplace.

## 6. The credibility-transfer play (and the compliance caution)

Your enterprise pedigree is your entire differentiator against every other solo AI consultant. Wield it — **ethically and within Adobe's rules.**

### How to invoke it (positioning, not client list)
- **Do say:** "I'm an engineering manager at Adobe building production AI systems for some of the largest companies in the world. I bring that same enterprise-grade approach to smaller businesses at a fraction of the cost."
- **Do say:** "I build AI for Fortune-500-scale operations for a living" / "enterprise AI infrastructure and generative models."
- **Do NOT say:** "I built [Coca-Cola / Home Depot / Ford / Tesco / Humain]'s AI." **Never name specific Adobe clients or projects.** That's Adobe's confidential relationship, likely covered by NDA, and it's the fastest way to a real problem. The *category* ("global enterprises," "Fortune 500") transfers the credibility without disclosing anything confidential.
- Frame the transfer as **methodology, not IP**: "the same rigor and architecture patterns used at enterprise scale, applied to your business." You're selling your *skill and judgment*, which are yours — not Adobe's IP.

### The compliance caution — CHECK THIS BEFORE YOU START (flag for Hooman)
This is a genuine risk area and **not legal advice** — you must verify against your actual Adobe agreements:
- **Moonlighting / outside-activity policy:** Many large employers require disclosure or approval of outside business activity. Check Adobe's employee handbook / code of business conduct.
- **Conflict of interest:** Your side clients must not overlap or compete with Adobe's business (Adobe sells AI/creative tooling broadly — this is a real edge to think about). SMB automation consulting is likely distinct, but confirm.
- **IP assignment / invention agreements:** Anything you build may be claimed by your employer if it relates to Adobe's business or is built on company time/resources. **Build your consulting work entirely on personal equipment, personal accounts, and personal time.** Never touch Adobe code, data, tools, or client relationships.
- **Use of employer name/brand:** Stating your *job title and employer factually* is generally fine (it's on your public LinkedIn); implying Adobe *endorses* your side business is not. Keep it as "I work at Adobe as an engineer," never "an Adobe service."
- **Non-solicitation:** Don't solicit Adobe clients, and don't use any contact or lead you obtained through Adobe.

**Action item for Hooman:** Read Adobe's outside-activities / conflict-of-interest / IP-assignment policies (or ask HR/legal) *before* taking the first dollar. Surface any blocker early. ## 7. Concrete 90-day launch sequence (week-by-week to first 1–3 clients)

Assumes ~8–12 hrs/week (part-time). The spine: **pick a niche → mine warm network → produce one proof asset → run warm + community in parallel → layer cold outbound → close.**

### Phase 0 — Weeks 1–2: Foundation & warm-list mining
- **Week 1:**
  - Verify Adobe outside-activity/IP policy (§6) — *gate before anything else.*
  - **Choose ONE vertical** to start (e.g., specialty manufacturing/trade shops — you already have domain reconnaissance from the CutLog/auto_research work; or professional services). Niche beats generalist for a solo cold-start.
  - Export LinkedIn connections; build `warm_list.csv` (§1). Target 40–80 warm names.
  - Optimize LinkedIn profile: headline = "I build enterprise AI at Adobe · now automating [vertical] businesses." Add a featured demo.
- **Week 2:**
  - Send **10–15 "advice ask" messages** to warmest owners + **10 specific-referral asks** to referral super-nodes (§1).
  - Post your **first LinkedIn piece** (an "AI hype vs reality for [vertical]" educational post — low-lift, establishes credibility).
  - Draft your service offering: **one fixed-scope package** ("AI automation audit + one automation built, $X, 3 weeks"). Fixed scope removes buyer friction.

### Phase 1 — Weeks 3–5: Proof + community + first calls
- Take the warm **advice calls** booked from week 2. On each, listen for a concrete pain; offer a **discounted or free first automation** to 1–2 of the best fits *in exchange for a case study + referral*. This manufactures your proof asset.
- **Build the first automation** (evenings/weekend). Record a **2-min video demo** using your video-gen skill (§4).
- Join **2–3 vertical Facebook groups / Slacks** (§3); start contributing value (no pitching).
- Continue **2 LinkedIn posts/week.** Publish the demo video the moment it's done.
- Attend **one** local/vertical meetup or Chamber event.

### Phase 2 — Weeks 6–9: Layer in cold outbound (now you have proof)
- Set up cold-email infra: **separate domain, warm it up now** (§2), Apollo/Clay list of **~100 hand-picked owners** in your niche.
- Launch a **4-touch personalized sequence** (Templates A–B) to 20–30/day max; run parallel **LinkedIn outreach** (Templates C–D) to the same or adjacent list. Use Clay + your own LLM skills for genuine per-row personalization — this is your edge.
- Keep posting; **turn each community win into a LinkedIn post.**
- Do **one referral-ask round** to everyone from Phase 0–1, now that you have a case study to point at.

### Phase 3 — Weeks 10–13: Close & compound
- You should now have: 1 completed (proof) project, a case study + demo video, an active warm/community presence, and ~100 cold touches producing ~3–10 replies → a few discovery calls.
- **Convert calls to paid projects.** Use fixed-scope package pricing. Aim: **1–3 paying clients by day 90.**
- **Ask every satisfied client for a referral and a testimonial** at the win moment (§1). This is what turns 90 days into a self-sustaining practice.
- Review your real funnel numbers vs the assumptions in §2 and recalibrate volume.

### 90-day success definition
- **Minimum:** 1 paying client + 1 case study + a warm referral pipeline that didn't exist on day 1.
- **Target:** 2–3 paying clients (≈ a full part-time load and a real second income), a repeatable warm+content engine, and Toptal application submitted as an additional inbound source.
