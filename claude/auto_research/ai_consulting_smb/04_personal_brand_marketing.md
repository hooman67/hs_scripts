## 1. Positioning & Personal Brand

### 1.1 The core problem: "AI consultant" is now noise
Since 2023 the market has been flooded with generic "AI consultants" — most are ex-marketers who watched YouTube, wrapped ChatGPT, and charge for prompt tips. SMBs are simultaneously *bombarded* with AI pitches and *deeply skeptical* that any of them can ship. **Hooman's job is not to prove AI is useful — it's to prove HE can actually build and ship it.** His differentiator is not "I know AI" (everyone claims that); it's **provenance + shipping ability**: he does this for Fortune-100s at real scale, for real production traffic. That is a credential 99% of the "AI consultant" crowd cannot fake. *(ASSUMED — market read.)*

### 1.2 Positioning statement (the crisp version)
Pick ONE and commit. Recommended:

> **"I help small and mid-sized businesses deploy the same production AI that Fortune-100 companies use — without a Fortune-100 budget or team."**

Variants to A/B in his headline / hero:
- **"Enterprise-grade AI, built for the businesses that can't hire Adobe."** ← his own phrasing; strongest, most memorable. Use it.
- **"Custom AI systems for SMBs — from the person who builds them for the world's biggest brands."**
- **"I turn 'we should use AI' into a working system your team uses on Monday."**

**Positioning formula** (fill-in-the-blank, standard consulting frame — ASSUMED):
> I help **[mid-market operators / $2M–$50M businesses]** who **[know AI matters but have no one to build it]** achieve **[a shipped, ROI-positive AI system]** through **[custom agentic workflows, image/video generation, and ML — the same stack I run for Fortune-100 brands].** Unlike **[generic AI consultants who deliver slide decks and ChatGPT wrappers]**, I **[ship production systems].**

### 1.3 Niche down — this is the highest-leverage decision
Broad "AI for SMBs" is weak positioning. The market rewards specificity. Meriwether niched an events business to "food and bourbon" and it became her competitive advantage; Garofalo niched to conversion copy. **The narrower the niche, the easier the marketing.** *(VERIFIED — foundr.com consulting guide: "Define a narrow focus… choosing a specific niche rather than a broad market" became consultants' competitive advantage.)*

Two axes to niche on — pick at least one, ideally lead with a **capability niche** and expand to a vertical later:

**A. Capability niche (plays directly to his rare skills):**
- **Generative visual content at scale** — product photography, ad/video variants, catalog imagery. This is his literal Firefly-world superpower and almost no "AI consultant" can do it. Massive SMB pain: e-commerce, real estate, agencies, local retail all need volume visual content and can't afford studios.
- **Agentic workflow automation** — "AI employees" for ops: intake, support triage, quoting, document processing. High willingness-to-pay, clear ROI.

**B. Vertical niche (easier referrals, tighter messaging):** e-commerce / DTC brands, marketing agencies (who resell his visual-gen work), professional services (law/accounting/clinics for document + intake automation), local multi-location retail/franchise.

**Recommendation (ASSUMED):** Lead with the **generative-content capability niche** ("I make Fortune-100-quality product & ad imagery/video for SMBs using the AI I build at Adobe") because (a) it's the least commoditized, (b) it's visually demonstrable — every deliverable is its own marketing asset, and (c) it directly leverages his Firefly-adjacent edge. Add **agentic automation** as the "and I also build the plumbing" upsell. Vertical-specialize once he's closed 2–3 clients in one sector.

### 1.4 Practice name vs. personal-name brand
**Recommendation: lead with the PERSONAL brand, register a light practice name as a wrapper.** *(ASSUMED — but supported: foundr guide notes new consultants win on "personal authority through blogging and community participation… more valuable initially than an elaborate firm name.")*

Rationale:
- His entire moat is *him* — the Adobe-scale credential is personal, non-transferable, and the reason anyone hires him. A faceless agency name throws that away.
- Solo founders who hide behind an agency name read as "probably one guy pretending to be a team" — which erodes trust. Owning "it's me, and that's the point" is stronger.
- A personal brand is portable if he later pivots, and it's what LinkedIn's algorithm rewards (person-profiles > company-pages for reach). *(ASSUMED.)*

**Practical structure:** Brand as **"Hooman Shariati — [tagline]"** in all human-facing copy. Register a simple practice name for the invoice/LLC/domain layer, e.g. a short mark like **"North Loop AI," "Shariati AI Studio," "Applied AI Works," "[Name] Labs."** Keep it forgettable-but-professional; it's a legal/billing wrapper, not the brand you market. Get a `.com` if the personal name domain (`hoomanshariati.com` / `shariati.ai`) is available — buy that too. Avoid cutesy names and avoid anything with "Adobe," "Firefly," or a client's name in it (see §7).

### 1.5 How to differentiate from the flood
Six concrete differentiators to hammer everywhere (ASSUMED, but these are the credible ones for him):
1. **Provenance** — "the AI I build powers [category leaders]" (worded carefully, §7). This is the single most powerful line; nobody in the SMB-consultant crowd has it.
2. **I ship, I don't advise** — offer *built systems*, not strategy decks. Anti-position against the deck-and-invoice crowd explicitly.
3. **Fixed-scope, fixed-price productized offers** — SMBs fear open-ended consulting bills. Package it (e.g. "AI Product-Photo Engine — $X, live in 2 weeks"). Reduces buyer risk, differentiates from hourly generalists.
4. **Show, don't tell** — his image/video-gen skill means every proof point is a *visible* before/after. Use it relentlessly (§4).
5. **Technical honesty** — he can say "here's what AI can't do for you yet," which builds trust generalists can't match.
6. **Right-sized** — "built for a 20-person company, not a 20,000-person one." Explicitly SMB-native.

## 2. Website

### 2.1 Reality check: the site is a *credibility anchor*, not a lead engine
For a solo B2B consultant, the website almost never *generates* leads cold. It **converts** leads sent from LinkedIn, referrals, and conversations — people who Google him or click his LinkedIn link. So: build it fast, make it credible, don't over-engineer. *(ASSUMED — standard solo-consultant funnel logic; consistent with foundr's finding that community/content marketing, not the website, drives clients.)*

### 2.2 What a high-converting solo-consultant site actually needs
Keep it to **one page (long-scroll) or ≤5 pages.** Structure, top to bottom:

1. **Hero (above the fold) — the ONE message.** Headline = the positioning statement. Subhead = who it's for + the outcome. ONE primary CTA button ("Book a free AI teardown" / "See if AI fits your business — 20-min call"). A photo of *him* (face = trust for a personal brand). No carousel, no jargon.
   - Example hero copy: **"Enterprise-grade AI, built for businesses that can't hire Adobe."** / sub: *"I build the same production AI systems used by Coca-Cola, Ford, and Home Depot — packaged so a 10-to-200-person company can afford and actually use them."* / button: **"Book a free 20-min AI teardown."**
2. **Proof strip / logos** — this is delicate given the Adobe conflict (§7). Instead of client logos, use: "10+ years building AI • Currently: AI platform infra & generative models at Adobe • Systems serving [scale metric]." Frame it as *his role*, not client endorsements.
3. **The offer(s)** — 2–3 productized packages with names, what's included, timeline, and "starting at $X" or "book to scope." Clarity of offer is the #1 conversion lever for consultants. *(ASSUMED.)*
4. **Case studies / portfolio** — the manufactured proof from §4. Before/after visuals, demo builds, a short Loom/video walkthrough. Even 2–3 self-initiated projects here beat zero.
5. **How it works** — 3 steps ("Teardown → Build → Handoff"). Kills the "consulting is vague and scary" objection.
6. **About** — the human + credential story (§1.1 provenance, worded per §7). Photo, 1 paragraph, why he does this.
7. **FAQ** — handles objections: "Isn't this expensive?" "Do I need technical staff?" "Is my data safe?" "How is this different from ChatGPT?"
8. **Final CTA + booking embed** — Cal.com or Calendly inline. One click to a call.

**The rule:** every section funnels to the same single CTA (book a call). Don't offer five different next steps.

Covered in depth in §4. Short version for the site: fill the case-study section with **self-initiated demo projects and before/afters** built specifically to be shown publicly. This is legitimate and common. A "spec" portfolio (work you did to demonstrate ability, on your own initiative, for fictional or volunteer clients) is a standard way creative/technical freelancers bootstrap.

Profiles with a good photo + 5+ skills get materially more engagement. *(VERIFIED — LinkedIn best-practices: face ~60% of frame; 5+ skills → 3× more connection requests.)* Add a **banner image** that states his value prop in one line (he can generate a clean one with his own tools).

### 3.2 Content cadence & post types (this is the real engine)
**Cadence (ASSUMED, realistic for a part-time solo):** 2–3 posts/week, sustainable > sporadic bursts. Comment meaningfully on 3–5 others' posts daily (10 min) — engagement drives more reach than posting for a small account. Reserve 15 min/day for DMs and replies. The "reach out with value to 10–15 people a day" tactic is explicitly cited as what works. *(VERIFIED — foundr.com.)*

**Post types that convert for B2B AI services** (rotate — ASSUMED, standard B2B playbook):
1. **Before/after demos** — "I rebuilt this local bakery's product photos with AI in 20 min. Before → after." Visual, shareable, proves ability. His single best format given his skills.
2. **Teardowns** — "Here's how I'd add AI to [common SMB workflow]." Free value, positions him as the builder.
3. **Contrarian/educational takes** — "Most 'AI consultants' sell you a ChatGPT wrapper. Here's what actually moves the needle for a small business." Differentiates him from the flood.
4. **Build-in-public** — short clips of him building a demo. Authenticity + proof of skill.
5. **Myth-busting / "AI can't do X yet"** — technical honesty builds trust.
6. **Client-outcome stories** — *once he has them*, framed as case studies.
7. **Behind-the-credential** (careful, §7) — insights about enterprise AI *generalized*, never client-specific or IP.

**Rule:** ~80% give (value/teach/demo), ~20% ask (offer/CTA). End value posts with a soft CTA occasionally, not every time.

**Format tips:** hook in first line (before the "…see more" fold), short lines, one idea per post, native video/images (his edge), a question or CTA at the end. Post when his audience is active (test AM weekdays).

## 4. Portfolio & Proof Without Client Permission

He cannot show Adobe/client work. This is a solvable, common problem. **Manufacture credible proof** — all of these are legitimate and self-owned:

### 4.1 Ranked proof-generation tactics
1. **Self-initiated demo builds ("spec work")** — pick 3–5 realistic SMB scenarios and build working demos: e.g. an AI product-photo pipeline, an agentic customer-intake bot, an ad-video generator. These are *his* IP, publicly shareable, and double as case studies + LinkedIn content. **Highest priority.**
2. **Free "AI teardowns" of local/real businesses** — take a real (permission-granted or clearly-hypothetical) small business, publicly show "here's where AI could save them X." Generates content, demonstrates thinking, and is a soft pitch. Offer a few *actual* free teardowns to seed testimonials. This can literally be his lead-gen offer AND his portfolio.
3. **Before/after visual gallery** — his generative-media skill means he can produce stunning before/after transformations (product shots, real-estate photos, ad creative) on public/sample inputs. Every one is a portfolio piece and a post. Cheap to produce, highly persuasive.
4. **Teardown / walkthrough videos (YouTube + short-form)** — screen-recorded build walkthroughs and "how I'd AI-ify this business" videos. Uses his image/video-gen skill for polish. Builds an evergreen proof library and SEO/discovery. (See §5 for effort ranking.)
5. **Open-source a useful tool** — ship a small, genuinely useful AI utility (an SMB-focused agent template, an image-batch tool). GitHub stars = credibility with technical buyers and a talking point. Medium effort, durable proof.
6. **Written teardowns / mini-case-studies** — even without a client, "How I would build an AI support agent for a dental practice" as a detailed public doc demonstrates competence.
7. **Early free/discounted clients for testimonials** — do 1–2 projects free or cheap explicitly to earn a named testimonial and a real case study. The fastest path from "no proof" to "social proof." Trade discount for a public quote + logo permission.

### 4.2 Turning demos into a flywheel
Each demo build → a LinkedIn before/after post → a teardown video → a website case study → a lead-magnet example. **Build once, repurpose 4×.** This is how a part-time solo keeps a content channel fed without it becoming a second job.

### 5.1 On Upwork specifically (he asked)
**Verdict: a *supplementary* channel, not the foundation — and slightly at odds with his positioning.** *(ASSUMED.)*
- **Pros:** real buyers with budget and intent; a fast way to land the first 1–2 paying gigs and testimonials when you have zero brand; good for building reviews.
- **Cons:** race-to-the-bottom pricing, commoditizes him next to $15/hr "AI consultants," takes a fee, and the "I build enterprise AI" premium positioning is *undermined* by bidding on marketplace gigs. It trains him to compete on price, not provenance.
- **Recommendation:** if he wants *fast first revenue + reviews*, do a short, deliberate Upwork sprint — target only high-budget, specific gigs (generative media, agentic automation), price at the top of the band, and use it purely to harvest 2–3 five-star reviews and case studies. Then graduate off it. Do **not** let Upwork become the identity. LinkedIn + referrals + teardowns is the real machine.

## 6. Sequencing — Minimum Viable Brand First (do NOT build a website for 3 months)

The failure mode for a technical founder is polishing tools instead of talking to clients. Guardrail: **he should be in a prospect conversation within 2 weeks, before the "real" website exists.**

### Phase 0 — Clear the runway (Days 1–3, blocking)
- ✅ **Verify Adobe employment agreement / COI & moonlighting policy** (§7). *This gates everything public.* If it restricts an active competing practice, adjust scope/timing (consult, don't advertise; or seek written approval) before broadcasting.
- Decide: personal brand + light practice name (§1.4). Buy `hoomanshariati.com` / `shariati.ai`.
- Lock the positioning statement + niche (§1.2–1.3).

### Phase 1 — Minimum Viable Brand (Week 1)
- **LinkedIn profile rewrite** (headline, About, photo, banner, skills) — his highest-ROI asset, do it first. *(COI-compliant version until cleared.)*
- **One-page site** (Carrd or a Framer one-pager) with hero, offer, one demo, booking link. Ship it ugly-but-clear; iterate later.
- **Booking link** (Cal.com/Calendly).
- **Lead magnet v1** (the SMB AI checklist — 1 day).
- **Build 1 flagship demo** (a before/after visual or a working agent) to have *something* to show.

### Phase 2 — Start talking to people (Week 2 — parallel with everything after)
- Post the first LinkedIn demo/teardown.
- **Reach out to 10–15 warm contacts** with a value-first message (free teardown offer). *(VERIFIED tactic.)*
- Do 2–3 **free teardowns** for real SMBs → seed testimonials + case studies.
- (Optional) launch the Upwork sprint if he wants fast first revenue.

### Phase 3 — Build the real assets (Weeks 3–6, while already selling)
- Upgrade to the **full Framer site** with 3 case studies (from demos + free teardowns).
- Establish the **2–3×/week LinkedIn cadence** + daily engagement.
- Start **short-form video** using his gen-media skill.
- Turn free-teardown clients into **named testimonials + logos**.

### Phase 4 — Scale the funnel (Month 2+)
- Newsletter, guest podcasts, local speaking.
- Productize a demo if services are saturating his time.
- Vertical-specialize based on which niche closed.

**The sequencing rule in one line:** *LinkedIn + one-pager + one demo + 15 outreach messages beats a perfect website every time.* Build the brand while selling, not before.

## 7. The Adobe-Conflict Caution (must-read, must-verify)

⚠️ **This is a real risk, not a formality. He must verify his actual Adobe employment agreement, IP-assignment clause, and moonlighting/conflict-of-interest policy before publicly launching a competing/adjacent practice.** I cannot see his contract; treat everything here as prompts to check, not legal advice. *(ASSUMED — standard tech-employer terms; NOT verified against his specific agreement.)*

### 7.1 The four exposures and how to avoid each
1. **Implying Adobe endorsement.** Never say or imply Adobe backs, sponsors, or is aware of his consulting. Frame credentials as *personal role/experience*: ✅ "I'm an engineering manager building AI platform infra at Adobe" (factual) vs. ❌ "Adobe-endorsed AI consultant" / using Adobe/Firefly logos. Add a light disclaimer where sensible: "Views/services my own; not affiliated with or endorsed by my employer."
2. **Exposing client or employer IP / confidential work.** He **cannot** name Adobe's clients (Coca-Cola, Ford, etc.) as *his* clients, show internal work, reveal architectures, models, data, or metrics that are Adobe's. This is why the portfolio must be **100% self-initiated demo work** (§4). When invoking scale/provenance, keep it *generalized and role-based* ("I build production generative-AI systems used by major global brands") — and even that phrasing should be checked against confidentiality terms. When in doubt, drop the specific brand names entirely and say "Fortune-100 brands."
3. **Moonlighting / outside-activity restrictions.** Many tech employers restrict outside work, especially in the same field. Adobe is an AI company; SMB AI consulting is *adjacent to his day job* — the highest-scrutiny category. **He likely needs to: (a) read the policy, (b) possibly disclose / seek written approval, (c) keep it clearly outside work hours/resources.** Do not use Adobe hardware, accounts, code, or time.
4. **Non-compete / IP assignment.** Some agreements assign IP created during employment or in the same field even off-hours, and/or restrict competing. His demos and any tools should be built cleanly on personal equipment/accounts, and he should confirm they aren't captured by an IP-assignment clause.

### 7.2 Concrete guardrails
- Get the employment agreement + COI policy in front of a lawyer or Adobe's ethics/HR channel **before** the profile advertises an active practice.
- Portfolio = only self-made, public-safe demos. No client names as his own, no internal assets.
- Provenance wording: default to "Fortune-100 / major global brands" not named clients; add the "my own views, not my employer's" disclaimer.
- All consulting on personal time, personal devices, personal accounts.
- Interim (pre-clearance) posture: build the *expertise* brand ("I build production AI") without publicly running an *active competing business*, until cleared.

> **Bottom line for Hooman:** The credential is his superpower, but it's also the source of legal risk. The safe play is to market *the skill and the demonstrated ability* (via self-built demos) heavily, and reference *the employer relationship* factually and minimally — after confirming his contract permits an adjacent side practice at all.
