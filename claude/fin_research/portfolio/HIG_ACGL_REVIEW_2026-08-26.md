# HIG and ACGL: Review of the “Newly Cheap, Defensive Insurer” Thesis

**Review date:** 2026-08-26  
**Market-data cutoff:** approximately 12:59 p.m. PDT  
**Question tested:** Is the HIG/ACGL pair newly cheap, buyable, defensive, a good stagflation holding, and a better implementation of the portfolio's underweight Financials sleeve than diversified insurance exposure?

## Verdict

The other agent found two legitimate insurers, but the conclusion is too confident.

- **“Newly cheap”: mostly unsupported.** HIG was only about 4% below its 52-week high and ACGL about 6% below. Both were well above their 52-week lows. They are inexpensive on earnings multiples, not beaten-down stocks.
- **“Buyable”: yes, but not obvious bargains.** Both generate strong returns on equity and repurchase shares. ACGL is the more compelling company-specific candidate at the current valuation; HIG is reasonably valued but not clearly cheap relative to its book value.
- **“Defensive”: only relative to high-beta equities.** Their businesses and reported betas may be less cyclical than many stocks, but both can suffer reserve surprises, catastrophe losses, investment losses, and equity drawdowns. ACGL also has meaningful mortgage-insurance exposure to a severe housing/economic downturn.
- **“Fits stagflation”: mixed, not clean.** Higher reinvestment yields and annual policy repricing can help insurers. Claim-cost inflation, social/legal inflation, weaker insurance demand, bond-price losses, credit stress, and mortgage defaults can hurt them. Neither is a pure stagflation hedge.
- **“Slots into Financials”: yes only after relabeling.** They fit an `Insurance / defensive compounders` sleeve, not a broad Financials sleeve. Being underweight to a self-selected target is not itself a reason to buy.
- **Implementation:** Retain **1.5% KIE + 1.0% BRK.B** as the cleaner default. If accepting active single-company underwriting and reserve risk, ACGL merits a watchlist or small carveout; HIG does not currently offer enough additional valuation advantage to justify replacing diversified exposure.

### Why the headline P/Es require adjustment

HIG's approximately 9.1x headline P/E is not a clean continuing-business multiple. Second-quarter GAAP EPS included $1.15 per share of income from discontinued operations associated with Hartford Funds, including a $251 million tax benefit. The company's core EPS of $3.42 excluded discontinued operations. The cleaner current valuation is approximately 10x continuing/core earnings, not 9.1x.

ACGL's approximately 7.9x GAAP P/E includes investment and other items that Arch excludes from its operating measure. Its last four reported operating EPS figures were $2.77, $2.98, $2.50, and $2.56, totaling $10.81. At $100.72, that is **9.3x operating earnings**. The excluded equity-method investment income is economically real, so 9.3x is conservative, but it is the cleaner underwriting comparison.

## HIG assessment

### What is genuinely attractive

- Trailing core earnings ROE was 18.7% at 2026-06-30.
- Book value per diluted share rose 17% year over year to $70.28; book value excluding AOCI rose 15% to $78.91.
- The company returned $615 million in the quarter, including $450 million of repurchases, and authorized a new $4.2 billion repurchase program—about 11% of the current market capitalization.
- Personal Insurance improved materially as earned pricing outpaced loss-cost trends. Its underlying combined ratio improved to 86.3 from 88.0.
- Higher invested assets and reinvestment yields support investment income over time.

### What the “cheap and defensive” label misses

- Core earnings increased only 1% year over year while core EPS rose 6%; the share-count reduction, rather than underlying profit growth, supplied much of the per-share increase.
- Business Insurance's underlying combined ratio worsened to 89.3 from 88.0. Middle & Large Business deteriorated to a 101.9 reported combined ratio and 95.3 underlying ratio.
- Favorable prior-year reserve development was still $111 million pre-tax, with reserve reductions in workers' compensation and other lines offset partly by reserve increases in general liability and commercial auto.
- Personal Insurance written premiums fell 7% amid competition. Employee Benefits' core earnings fell 15% and its loss ratio worsened due to disability claims.
- The reported investment-income increase included $114 million from limited partnerships and alternatives versus $13 million a year earlier. That part should not be annualized as a stable bond yield.
- HIG trades at about 1.99x reported book and 1.77x book excluding AOCI, versus KIE's 1.62x aggregate book multiple. The earnings discount is not accompanied by a book-value discount.
- Repurchases above book value reduce book value per share at purchase, which the company explicitly acknowledged. They can still create value if future returns remain high, but the authorization is not free value.

### HIG verdict

**WATCH / reasonable value, not newly cheap.** HIG is a good insurer with strong capital returns, but the current price already assumes that high-teens core ROE and favorable personal-lines improvement persist. It is not a superior defensive purchase merely because the headline P/E is below 10.

## ACGL assessment

### What is genuinely attractive

- Book value per share was $68.04 at 2026-06-30, up 15% year over year and 2.8% in the quarter.
- The current price is about 1.48x reported book and 1.56x estimated tangible book—below KIE's 1.62x aggregate price/book ratio.
- Last-four-quarter operating EPS totals $10.81, producing a 9.3x operating multiple at the current price.
- Second-quarter annualized operating ROE was 15.3%, while net-income ROE was 18.0%.
- The company remains highly profitable across specialty insurance, reinsurance, and mortgage insurance. Its consolidated combined ratio excluding catastrophes and prior-year development was an excellent 82.5%.
- Arch repurchased $1.9 billion of shares in the first half of 2026, including $1.2 billion in the second quarter. Diluted weighted shares fell about 8% year over year in the second quarter.
- The balance sheet had $23.2 billion of common equity and $2.2 billion of remaining repurchase authorization at quarter end.

### What the “cheap and defensive” label misses

- Operating ROE fell to 15.3% from 18.2% a year earlier, and operating EPS was nearly flat at $2.56 versus $2.58.
- Net premiums written fell 6.9% overall: 5.1% in insurance and 10.4% in reinsurance. Some of that reflects disciplined non-renewals, reduced shares, and more retrocession, but it is also evidence that the extraordinary hard-market growth phase is moderating.
- Consolidated underwriting income fell 20%, and the combined ratio excluding catastrophe and reserve development worsened by 1.6 points to 82.5%.
- The quarter contained $165 million of favorable prior-year reserve development. Mortgage's exceptionally low 22.8 combined ratio included 15.7 points of favorable reserve development, largely from better-than-expected cures.
- Arch's own modeled Florida wind probable maximum loss was $1.8 billion, or 8% of tangible equity, as of 2026-07-01. Actual catastrophe losses can exceed models.
- Mortgage insurance is explicitly exposed to unemployment, home-price declines, and severe economic downturns. Arch modeled a severe-event mortgage loss of $931 million at 2026-01-01, although that was only 4.1% of tangible equity.
- Arch's 2025 annual report states that elevated inflation can materially affect long-tail reserve adequacy and investment values, and the final effect is unknowable until claims settle.
- ACGL pays no regular dividend; the shareholder-return thesis depends on book-value compounding and opportunistic repurchases.

### ACGL verdict

**WATCH / best of the two, but cyclical quality rather than defense.** ACGL offers a real valuation advantage and unusually strong underwriting, but the margin trend and premium contraction say not to capitalize peak-cycle earnings indefinitely. It is suitable only as risk capital with explicit catastrophe, reserve, and mortgage-cycle tolerance.

## Is insurance a stagflation hedge?

It has both positive and negative channels.

Positive:

- Premiums can be repriced at renewal, especially in short-tail lines.
- Insurers continually reinvest premium float, so sustained higher yields can lift investment income.
- Essential commercial and personal coverage can make revenue less economically sensitive than many discretionary businesses.

Negative:

- Inflation raises auto-parts, labor, construction, medical, litigation, and settlement costs—sometimes after the premium was fixed and years before a long-tail claim is paid.
- Higher rates reduce the market value of existing bonds and can contribute to recession, weaker demand, credit losses, and mortgage defaults.
- Catastrophes and reserve revisions can overwhelm the benefit from investment yield in any one period.
- Competition can force premium rates to moderate just as old loss inflation is emerging.

HIG's own annual report says rising inflation can increase loss costs and claim incidence and describes inflation as a direct P&C risk. Arch similarly warns that inflation may materially affect long-tail reserve adequacy. The evidence therefore supports **partial inflation adaptation**, not “stagflation hedge.”

## Portfolio implementation decision

The user's 2.5% sleeve is too small to require both active stocks plus BRK.B. The main choices are:

1. **Default: 1.5% KIE + 1.0% BRK.B.** This preserves the intended non-bank insurance/defensive-compounder exposure without making the result depend on one company's reserves. KIE owns 53 modified-equal-weight insurance names and already includes HIG and ACGL.
2. **Active carveout: 0.75% ACGL + 0.75% KIE + 1.0% BRK.B.** Use only if accepting ACGL's catastrophe, reserve, reinsurance-cycle, and mortgage risk. This makes ACGL meaningful without allowing one underwriting judgment to dominate the sleeve.
3. **Two-stock version: 0.75% HIG + 0.75% ACGL + 1.0% BRK.B.** This is defensible as a deliberately active portfolio, but it is not clearly superior to KIE and adds monitoring requirements.

Do not add either stock merely to eliminate a percentage-point drift. First confirm the live holdings, account, tax lot, and source of funds. The portfolio still has a separate instruction not to deploy recession dry powder to equities.

## Monitoring triggers

For HIG:

- Business Insurance underlying combined ratio, especially Middle & Large;
- general-liability and commercial-auto reserve development;
- personal-lines pricing relative to loss-cost inflation;
- disability loss ratio;
- continuing-business earnings after Hartford Funds closes;
- repurchase price relative to book value and normalized ROE.

For ACGL:

- insurance and reinsurance renewal pricing and net-premium contraction;
- combined ratio excluding catastrophes and prior-year development;
- favorable reserve development as a share of earnings;
- mortgage delinquencies, cures, unemployment, and home prices;
- catastrophe exposure relative to tangible equity;

## Final decision

The insurer idea is worth **continuing to research**, especially ACGL, but it is not the cleanest immediate buy. The cleanest sleeve implementation remains diversified insurance plus BRK.B. If the goal is specifically to exploit a company-level valuation, ACGL is the better candidate; HIG should remain on watch until either its price offers a wider margin of safety or its continuing-business earnings after the Hartford Funds sale establish a cleaner base.
