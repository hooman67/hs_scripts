## What an 8% Bond Yield Means

### The current market

**VERIFIED as of 2026-10-05:** broad U.S. investment-grade corporate bonds yielded approximately 6.03%,
while broad U.S. high-yield bonds yielded approximately 8.15%. The 8% opportunity is therefore real, but
it is mostly compensation for credit risk rather than a special arbitrage.

High-yield bondholders face:

- defaults and recoveries below par;
- equity-like drawdowns when recessions increase expected defaults;
- ordinary-income taxation on most distributions;
- liquidity and bid/ask deterioration during stress; and
- call risk, because issuers refinance bonds that become attractive to the investor.

A simplified illustration shows why yield is not return: if 4% of a portfolio defaults during a bad
credit year and recovery is 40%, expected principal loss is approximately 2.4% before fees. Defaults are
not smooth annual events; they cluster when the investor is least willing to absorb them.

For an individual bond advertised at 8%, verify **yield to worst**, not merely coupon or current yield,
plus maturity, call schedule, price versus par, seniority, rating/watch status, accrued interest, odd-lot
markup and the dealer's bid/ask spread.

### Tax-adjusted context

At the modeled 22% federal plus 9.3% California marginal rate, ordinary interest receives an
approximately 31.3% tax haircut:

- 8.15% high-yield bond income becomes approximately **5.60% after current tax**, before defaults and
  price changes;
- 6.03% investment-grade income becomes approximately **4.14% after current tax**; and
- CMF's approximately 3.83% federal-and-California-exempt SEC yield is roughly equivalent to **5.58% of
  fully taxable income** at that marginal rate.

CMF is therefore more competitive than its headline yield suggests, but its California concentration and
duration prevent it from being treated as cash.

**IBDU or a diversified 2028–2030 investment-grade ladder**

**JAAA** can provide floating-rate, high-quality structured-credit income with low observed volatility.

**USHY** is the preferred diversified route to approximately 8% portfolio YTM because its 0.08% expense

The yield should not be chased before a recession merely because the coupon looks attractive.

If later approved, cap speculative credit at approximately **1%–2% initially**, classify it as cyclical
risk, and do not count it toward house-safe assets. High yield tends to fall with equities precisely when
VGIT is intended to help.

## Tax Comparison

### California: direct Treasuries versus VGIT and TLT

For a California taxable account, the interest component is treated almost identically. Interest from
a directly held U.S. Treasury is exempt from California income tax. California also exempts the portion
of a qualifying fund's dividend attributable to U.S. government obligations when at least 50% of the
fund's assets meet the state's test. The latest completed-year sponsor reports show **VGIT at 100.00%**
and **TLT at 99.64%** U.S.-government-source income for 2025. Thus, VGIT matched direct Treasury interest
for this purpose and TLT was only fractionally less exempt. These percentages can change annually and
must be taken from the sponsor's year-end tax supplement.

The exemption does not extend to capital gains from selling either ETF or a Treasury before maturity;
California taxes those gains under its normal income-tax rules. Federally, interest and ordinary ETF
distributions remain taxable, while sale gains or losses follow the applicable capital-gain rules.
Direct Treasuries avoid a fund expense ratio and provide a stated maturity, but purchases at a premium
or discount can create additional tax-basis and reporting details. The material choice between TLT,
VGIT and a direct Treasury is therefore duration, maturity certainty, liquidity and cost—not California
tax treatment.

For comparison, the 2025 sponsor reports show **SGOV at 95.14%** U.S.-government-source income. It
passes California's 50% fund-asset threshold, so 95.14% of the reported ordinary dividend is generally
eligible for the California subtraction and 4.86% remains state-taxable. **VCIT reported only 0.41%**
U.S.-government-source income and does not pass California's 50% threshold; its corporate-bond income
is therefore effectively fully taxable by California. The tax gap between SGOV and a direct Treasury
is small, while the gap between VCIT and Treasury exposure is material.

### Why the viral comparison is invalid

The cited **$8,770** is exactly the estimated 2026 federal income tax on **$80,000 of gross W-2 wages** for
a single filer using the $16,100 standard deduction. It excludes California income tax and payroll tax.

Comparing that tax with the tax on distributions from an **$80,000 investment principal** mixes two
different denominators:

- $80,000 of wages is earned income;
- $80,000 invested in an ETF is capital that produces only approximately $8,000–$11,500 of annual cash
  distributions; and
- much of QQQI/GPIQ's distribution is currently classified as return of the investor's own tax basis.

The appropriate comparison uses the same invested principal and measures the incremental tax on each
fund's distribution.

Assumptions: $80,000 gross W-2 wages, single California resident, standard deductions, no other income or
deductions, no NIIT, 22% federal marginal ordinary rate, 15% qualified-dividend/long-term-gain rate, and
9.3% California marginal rate. California taxes qualified dividends and capital gains as ordinary income.
The latest published 2025 California schedule is used because a final 2026 schedule was not located.

| Fund | Annual cash distribution | Currently taxable character | Incremental federal tax | Incremental CA tax | Total current tax | Cash after current tax |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **QQQI** | $14,340 | $57.73 QDI + $14,282.27 ROC | $8.66 | $5.37 | **$14.03** | $14,325.97 |
| **GPIQ** | $9,940 | $432.89 ordinary + $9,507.11 ROC | $95.24 | $40.26 | **$135.49** | $9,804.51 |
| **JEPQ** | $10,690 | $10,153.91 nonqualified + $536.09 QDI | $2,314.27 | $994.17 | **$3,308.44** | $7,381.56 |

Divide every dollar amount by ten for a $10,000 investment. The resulting current taxes are approximately
$1.40 for QQQI, $13.55 for GPIQ and $330.84 for JEPQ per $10,000 invested.

The modeled worker's baseline tax is approximately $8,770 federal plus $3,195 California, or $11,965,
before FICA and California payroll deductions. This baseline is not caused by the ETF investment and
should not be placed beside an ETF's incremental distribution tax as though the figures represent equal
economic income.

### Return of capital is deferral, not free income

Return of capital normally reduces cost basis dollar-for-dollar. After basis reaches zero, additional
return of capital becomes taxable capital gain; selling the shares earlier generally produces a larger
gain or smaller loss because basis has been reduced.

For the modeled $100,000 QQQI investment, one year of unchanged tax character would reduce basis by about
$14,282. If the fund's share value did not create an equivalent economic return, part of the attractive
cash distribution would literally be returned principal. Even when the fund does earn the distribution,
the near-zero current tax is still primarily **tax deferral** into a future sale or zero-basis gain.

Section 1256 index-option treatment can help option strategies because gains are normally 60% long-term
and 40% short-term federally. At the modeled brackets that would be approximately 17.8% federal plus 9.3%
California. However, the latest final distribution character for these funds showed no distributed
Section 1256 or long-term capital gain, so that hypothetical treatment should not be substituted for the
actual tax forms.

  income, and a high distribution can reduce compounding or merely return basis.
- Do not interpret 8% as guaranteed return. For credit funds, evaluate yield to worst, spread, defaults,
  recovery, fees, tax and price change together.
