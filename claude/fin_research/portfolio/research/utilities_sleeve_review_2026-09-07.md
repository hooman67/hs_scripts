## Why a sleeve still belongs

### The case for keeping 2.5%

- **Different earnings pattern from Adobe/technology.** Regulated utilities earn approved returns on infrastructure rather than depending primarily on advertising, software budgets, or high-multiple terminal growth.
- **Lower equity-market sensitivity.** Over the five years ended 2026-09-04, VPU's adjusted daily returns had approximately **0.45 correlation** and **0.44 beta** to VTI in this review's calculation.
- **Real cash distributions.** Current broad-utility ETF yields are roughly 2.7%-2.9%. At 2.5%, VPU would generate about **$468 annual gross distributions** at a 2.83% indicated yield, versus roughly $71 from the current XLU position using XLU's 2.85% SEC yield.
- **Electric-load growth.** Data centers, manufacturing, electrification, and grid replacement can support rate-base and generation growth. This is a real tailwind, although regulators and financing determine how much reaches shareholders.
- **Complements rather than duplicates the other power sleeves.** VPU is predominantly electric and multi-utilities; GEV/ETN are equipment suppliers, while CCJ/BWXT/CEG/NLR implement uranium and nuclear exposure.

### Why 4% is too large

- The accepted targets already commit **3.5% to uranium/nuclear** and **1.5% to grid/electrification**. Keeping 4% utilities would make the direct power complex **9.0%** before utilities already embedded in broad index funds.
- At target weights, VTI, VXUS and VWO contribute approximately **0.63 percentage points** of utilities look-through: `12% VTI × 2.5% + 8% VXUS × 3.3% + 2.5% VWO × 2.8%`.
- A 4% utility sleeve would therefore take the portfolio to roughly **9.6% power-related exposure before incidental overlaps elsewhere**. The accepted 2.5% target reduces that to roughly **8.1%**, including broad-index utility look-through, still a meaningful thematic overweight.
- Utilities are capital-intensive equities. They can lose one-third of their value in an equity shock, so extra utilities do not solve the 3-5-year house-capital problem.
- The reduction costs little income. At today's indicated VPU yield, 4.0% would produce about $750 gross annually and 2.5% about $468—a difference of only about **$281 per year**.

## What utilities actually hedge

| Regime | Expected behavior | Portfolio implication |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary slowdown/disinflation | Stable demand and falling yields often help relative performance | Useful diversifier |
| Severe equity/liquidity shock | Still equity; can fall with the market | **Not** house-capital protection |
| Moderate inflation with constructive regulation | Costs and capex may eventually enter rate base | Partial, delayed pass-through |
| Stagflation/rising real yields | Financing costs rise before rate relief; allowed returns reset with lag | Weak/ambiguous hedge |
| AI/data-center load boom | More generation, transmission and distribution investment | Positive demand, but regulated returns cap upside and capex must be financed |
| Wildfire/extreme weather | Restoration costs, liability, disallowance and customer-affordability risk | Material downside for exposed issuers |

The historical evidence rejects the phrase “recession hedge” if it means capital protection:

- From 2020-02-19 through 2020-03-23, VPU fell approximately **35.8%**, XLU fell 35.4%, and VTI fell 35.0%; IEF gained about 6.4%.
- In calendar 2022, when rates and inflation hurt both broad equities and Treasuries, VPU gained about 2.0% and XLU 2.5%, versus VTI at -20.0% and IEF at -14.4%.
- Over the five years through 2026-09-04, VPU's maximum drawdown was approximately **25.2%** despite its low market beta.

Utilities are therefore **defensive equity with regime-diversification value**, not a substitute for cash, insured deposits, short Treasuries, or currency-matched house reserves.

## Business-quality risks inside the basket

### Regulated stability is real, but not bond-like

Regulated utilities generally receive monopoly service territories and authorized returns on prudently invested rate base. That can make earnings and dividends more predictable than cyclical industrial or technology earnings. The tradeoff is that growth requires continual capital investment and external financing.

Key risks are:

- **Regulatory lag:** fuel, labor, storm and interest costs can rise before a rate case permits recovery.
- **Financing:** a 4.78% 10-year Treasury yield on 2026-09-04 raises debt and equity costs for long-duration, capital-intensive companies.
- **Affordability/politics:** rapid data-center capex can provoke disputes over whether residential customers subsidize large-load customers.
- **Execution:** large transmission, generation and interconnection projects can run late or over budget.
- **Wildfire/weather:** XLU's 2026-09-03 holdings included PG&E at 2.29% and Edison International at 1.62%; diversification limits but does not remove liability and regulatory-disallowance risk.
- **Dividend risk:** utility dividends are usually steadier than market earnings, but leverage, adverse regulation, disasters or major capex can still slow or cut payouts.

### AI demand does not automatically equal shareholder returns

The U.S. Department of Energy/Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated data centers used approximately 4.4% of U.S. electricity in 2023 and could reach roughly 6.7%-12% by 2028. This supports the load-growth thesis. It does not establish that every utility earns excess returns:

- regulated utilities need commission approval and usually earn capped returns on approved investment;
- merchant generators such as CEG and VST capture more power-price and contract upside but bring commodity, outage, leverage and contract risk;
- equipment makers such as GEV and ETN capture grid spending but are industrial growth equities with different valuations and cyclicality.

The separate sleeves are therefore economically sensible.

### Why not the others

- **XLU:** excellent ETF, but the portfolio does not need institutional options liquidity. Its 31-stock, large-cap-only basket concentrates NEE and adds more CEG overlap.

- **UTES:** recent performance was strong, but the fund is active, concentrated, lower-yielding and heavily exposed to merchant generators already represented by CEG/NLR. That is a different, higher-beta mandate—not the desired defensive regulated-utility sleeve.
- **IDU/JXI and Canadian utility ETFs:** they add cost, concentration, currency/account complications, or U.S.-tax PFIC risk without solving a portfolio gap. A CAD trading currency would not remove the underlying economic currency exposure.

## Account and tax implementation

- All five compared ETFs are **U.S.-domiciled, USD-listed 1940 Act funds**, so none creates commodity-pool K-1 treatment.
- For a U.S. tax resident, a U.S. taxable brokerage account is the cleanest default for new VPU purchases. Broad utility distributions are generally eligible for qualified-dividend treatment to the extent reported by the fund and holding-period requirements are met; verify the year-end tax statement.
- Holding VPU in a Canadian USD subaccount does not diversify away from USD. The assets and operating cash flows remain primarily U.S.-dollar exposure.
- Avoid substituting a Canadian-domiciled utilities ETF in a U.S. taxable account without cross-border advice; foreign pooled funds commonly create PFIC/Form 8621 complexity for U.S. taxpayers.
- An RRSP can receive U.S. treaty treatment federally, but U.S. state conformity, reporting and eventual distribution taxation vary. Do not use RRSP room solely to shelter a roughly 2.8% yield without coordinated cross-border tax advice.
- Selling XLU at a small loss and buying VPU is commonly treated as a defensible tax-loss swap because the funds follow different indexes and have materially different breadth, but the IRS has not published an ETF-by-ETF safe list. Avoid repurchasing XLU for at least 31 days and confirm with the tax preparer if claiming the loss.

Review quarterly, not daily. Revisit the 2.5% target if:

- the aggregate utilities + uranium/nuclear + grid allocation exceeds **8.5%** through appreciation;

Rebalance VPU around a **2.0%-3.0% band**. Do not use it as the source of emergency or house-purchase liquidity.
