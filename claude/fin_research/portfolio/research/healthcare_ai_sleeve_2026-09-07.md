Broad healthcare plus a device tilt is the investable core; any pure AI-drug bet belongs inside the existing 1% speculation cap, not in this sleeve.

## Lilly: should it be bought back?

### What improved fundamentally

Lilly's 2026 second quarter was exceptionally strong:

- Revenue rose **48% to $23.0 billion**.
- Reported EPS rose 26%; adjusted EPS rose **33% to $8.38**.
- Mounjaro revenue was $9.94 billion and Zepbound revenue was $4.93 billion. Together they represented **64.7% of quarterly revenue**.
- Lilly raised full-year revenue guidance to **$85-$87 billion** and guided adjusted EPS to **$35.50-$36.50** after absorbing acquired IPR&D charges.
- Foundayo/orforglipron began contributing revenue, and Lilly reported positive results from three additional Phase 3 retatrutide obesity trials, completing the clinical data package for global registrations.

This is not a broken growth story. Lilly remains the best large-cap drug-development compounder in the current set.

### Why it is still not an automatic buy

At the 2026-09-04 close of **$1,149.36**:

- Price / midpoint of company 2026 adjusted-EPS guidance is about **31.9x**.
- Price / current 2027 analyst-consensus EPS of about $47.26 is about **24.3x**. This estimate is not company guidance and can change materially.
- The stock is about **11% below its 52-week high**, but only about **7% below Hooman's $1,235.56 sale price**. That is a pullback, not yet a valuation reset.
- Mounjaro and Zepbound supply almost two-thirds of quarterly revenue. The same concentration that creates extraordinary growth also raises pricing, reimbursement, safety, manufacturing and competitive risk.
- Quarterly volume rose 60%, but realized prices fell 13%. Future growth depends on volume continuing to overwhelm price pressure.
- Competition is expanding across injectable and oral incretins and next-generation obesity drugs. Retatrutide and Foundayo strengthen Lilly's position, but their success is increasingly embedded in expectations.
- Lilly's SEC filing acknowledges AI collaborations and operational use, but it does not disclose AI-originated drug revenue. Lilly is a drug-franchise investment, not a measurable AI pure play.

### Re-entry rule

**Verdict: wait; regain indirect exposure through VHT/XLV now.** Set a maximum direct target of **1% of portfolio, about $6,620 or roughly six shares** at current scale.

Use valuation-linked tranches, checked against updated earnings estimates at each purchase:

| Trigger | Approx. valuation using $36 2026 guidance / $47.26 2027 consensus | Action |
|---|---:|---|
| **$1,050 or lower** | 29.2x / 22.2x | Buy 2 shares if Q3/Q4 estimates and GLP-1 share remain intact |
| **$950 or lower** | 26.4x / 20.1x | Buy 2 more shares |
| **$850 or lower** | 23.6x / 18.0x | Buy final 2 shares, unless the decline reflects a thesis break |

An equivalent rule is better than anchoring to fixed prices: begin around **22x next-twelve-month earnings** and become more aggressive near 20x, provided estimates have not fallen. Do not buy merely because the price is below the prior sale.

Stop the re-entry if growth deteriorates for fundamental reasons: material GLP-1 share loss, a safety signal, sustained failure of capacity expansion, weak Foundayo/retatrutide execution, or revenue estimates falling enough that the apparent multiple discount disappears.

## Pfizer: sell, trim, or retain 1%?

### Current evidence

Pfizer is not devoid of upside:

- Q2 revenue was **$15.0 billion**, up 3% reported and 1% operational; excluding COVID products, revenue rose 5% operationally.
- Recently launched and acquired products grew **18% operationally**, with Padcev and the Seagen oncology assets contributing.
- Pfizer raised the midpoint of 2026 revenue guidance to **$60.5-$62.5 billion** and reaffirmed adjusted EPS of **$2.80-$3.00**.
- At $28.45, the stock is about **9.8x** the guidance midpoint and the $1.76 current annual dividend rate yields about **6.2%**.

But it remains mismatched to the stated objective:

- Q2 adjusted EPS was flat year over year, and current consensus expects roughly flat-to-lower revenue and EPS into 2027.
- Pfizer itself forecasts a **$1.5 billion revenue impact from patent/exclusivity losses in 2026**, with the rate of revenue loss expected to accelerate through 2030.
- The quarter included **$4.3 billion of non-cash intangible impairments**, a reminder that acquired pipeline value is not guaranteed.
- Pfizer paid $4.9 billion of dividends in the first half, is prioritizing deleveraging, and does not expect 2026 buybacks. The dividend is support, not growth.
- Pfizer is already about 2.6% of XLV and 2.1% of VHT. A broad core preserves a small diversified Pfizer exposure even after the direct position is sold.

### Decision

**Default recommendation: sell the direct PFE position rather than retain 1%.** The accepted dividend rule says a dividend is a bonus only after growth, quality, valuation and role independently pass. Pfizer passes valuation and income, but not the growth/AI role Hooman wants from this sleeve.

Retaining **up to 1% / about 233 shares** remains defensible only if Hooman explicitly wants a value-income barbell and accepts the patent-cliff risk. It is not required for diversification because the broad ETF already owns Pfizer.

At the live average book cost of $25.28 and price of $28.45, selling all 738 shares would realize an aggregate gain of about **$2,339** before lot-specific adjustments. Verify account and lot dates first:

- **RRSP:** an outright sale is operationally clean; under the usual U.S.-Canada treaty treatment, current income/gains inside a qualifying RRSP are generally tax-deferred for a U.S. resident, subject to proper reporting.
- **U.S. taxable:** prefer long-term-gain lots where possible. The difference between short- and long-term treatment may justify staging, but not remaining structurally overweight indefinitely.
- **Canadian non-registered account while U.S.-resident:** obtain cross-border tax advice; U.S. worldwide taxation and foreign-account reporting apply, and Canadian-domiciled ETFs may create PFIC complications. Use U.S.-listed ETFs for this sleeve.
- A covered call is not a tax cure. Use it only if the strike is an acceptable sale price and assignment is welcome; do not delay an approved exit merely to collect a small premium.

Selling PFE replaces roughly **$1,299/year** of current-rate dividends with lower-yielding growth assets. The approximate income reduction is under $1,000/year, less than 0.15% of the portfolio, and is consistent with the growth-first objective.

This is a cleaner expression of the thesis than 2.5% XLV + 1.5% GEHC + 1% PFE:

- It replaces the low-growth legacy PFE position.
- It avoids making 30% of the sleeve depend on one moderate-growth device company.
- It still gives targeted device exposure while retaining drug-development, biotech, tools and payer exposure.
- It accepts that Lilly is the strongest growth name, but caps direct concentration and waits for valuation.
- At current VHT weights, a 3% VHT position already adds about **0.40% indirect LLY**. A fully funded 1% direct LLY position would therefore create roughly **1.4% total Lilly look-through**, not merely 1%.

The fund fees on 3% VHT + 1% IHI are only about **$42/year** on the current portfolio. Using XLV instead of VHT saves roughly $2/year; breadth, not fees, should decide.

### Acceptable alternatives

- **Use XLV instead of VHT** if maximum liquidity, tight spreads or options are important. Do not own both.
- **Use 1% GEHC instead of 1% IHI** if Hooman deliberately prefers valuation and direct AI-imaging concentration over diversification. Do not own both unless each is reduced; they overlap.
- **Use 0.5% IBB by reducing VHT to 2.5%** only if Hooman explicitly wants extra biotechnology volatility. IBB is not proven AI exposure, and the 0.44% fee buys a tilt rather than a new asset class.
- **Do not use XBI, ARKG, HTEC, RXRX, SDGR or TEM in the core.** A pure-play position would compete with LULU and any options risk for space inside the separate 1% speculation cap.

## Explicit answers

- **Should Lilly be bought back now?** No. The business is excellent, but a 7% decline from the sale price has not created enough valuation margin. Re-enter in stages beginning near $1,050 or about 22x current next-year consensus, after verifying estimates.
- **Are XLV and GEHC still the best options?** XLV remains an excellent core, but VHT is marginally better for this long-term, breadth-first thesis. GEHC remains a good value satellite, but IHI is the better default for an investor who prefers diversification over single-name risk.
- **Are they better than Lilly?** They solve different problems. VHT/XLV is the core; IHI/GEHC is the device/diagnostics expression; Lilly is a valuation-sensitive drug-growth satellite. None is a one-for-one substitute for the others.
- **Is there a better AI-biotech instrument?** Not a clean one. IBB is the least-bad liquid biotech tilt, but it is biotechnology exposure rather than demonstrated AI economics. Pure-play AI names remain speculative and should not displace profitable core holdings.
- **What should happen to Pfizer?** Sell the direct position by default. Keep at most 1% only as an explicit value-income exception, not as part of the AI-growth thesis.
