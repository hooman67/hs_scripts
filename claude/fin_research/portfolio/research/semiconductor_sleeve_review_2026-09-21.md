# Semiconductor Exposure Review — 2026-09-21

## Decision

Semiconductors are one of the strongest long-run structural growth industries, but a dedicated
semiconductor sleeve is **not currently necessary** and the major semiconductor ETFs are **not attractive
enough to chase now**.

The portfolio's permanent semiconductor exposure should come primarily through the planned VTI, VXUS,
VWO and JQUA allocations. Once those sleeves are funded, semiconductor companies should represent roughly
**3.3%–3.8% of the total portfolio** on a look-through basis. A dedicated fund would therefore be an
intentional overweight, not the repair of a missing exposure.

If a better entry develops, permit a **0.5% initial tactical overweight and a 1.0% maximum** using SOXQ.
Fund it by substituting for part of VTI/JQUA rather than adding another permanent sleeve on top of the
allocation. Do not buy AMD or Intel directly at current prices.

No portfolio or allocation change was made as part of this review.

## What the Semiconductor Sector Includes

Yes, both **AMD and Intel are semiconductor companies**, but they represent different business models:

- **Fabless designers:** Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Qualcomm and Marvell design chips and outsource most
  manufacturing.
- **Foundries:** TSMC and GlobalFoundries manufacture chips designed by other companies.
- **Integrated device manufacturers:** Intel, Micron, Texas Instruments and Analog Devices combine varying
  degrees of design and manufacturing.
- **Semiconductor equipment:** ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research and KLA sell the tools required to
  manufacture leading-edge chips.
- **Design infrastructure and specialty components:** Arm, Synopsys, Cadence, Teradyne, Entegris and other
  suppliers support the design, testing and production chain. Index inclusion varies by methodology.

Amazon designs Graviton and Trainium chips for its own cloud but is not a semiconductor-sector company;
its economics remain principally cloud, retail and advertising. Eaton and GE Vernova benefit from the
electricity and infrastructure demanded by chip fabrication and AI data centers, but they are also not
direct semiconductor exposure.

## Is the Industry Worth Owning?

### Structural case: strong

- AI training and inference require accelerators, networking, memory, packaging and fabrication capacity.
- Semiconductor content continues to increase in vehicles, industrial automation, defense, telecom and
  consumer devices.
- Leading foundries and equipment suppliers have exceptionally difficult-to-replicate technology,
  capital, intellectual property and customer relationships.
- The industry's strongest companies have real revenue, profits and free cash flow. This is not merely a
  pre-revenue thematic basket.

WSTS's Spring 2026 forecast projects the global semiconductor market to grow approximately **90% in 2026
to $1.51 trillion**, followed by another **27% in 2027**. Memory is forecast to rise roughly **250% in
2026**, while logic grows approximately 37%.

### Cyclical case: unusually dangerous now

Those same numbers are a warning. A 90% industry-growth year, driven disproportionately by a 250% memory
surge, is not a normalized run rate. Semiconductors remain exposed to:

- inventory corrections and abrupt memory-price reversals;
- hyperscaler capital-expenditure digestion if AI monetization disappoints;
- customer concentration and circular AI financing;
- U.S.–China export controls and Taiwan geopolitical risk;
- high fixed costs and operating leverage at foundries and manufacturers;
- rapid product obsolescence; and
- expensive acquisitions or excessive capacity additions near cycle peaks.

The industry deserves permanent exposure, but the **dedicated overweight should be tactical** because the
broad portfolio already owns the sector and current conditions resemble a powerful cyclical upswing.

## Why SOXQ Is the Best Vehicle

SOXQ tracks the widely cited PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index, commonly called the **SOX Index**. The index
selects the 30 largest eligible U.S.-listed semiconductor and production-equipment companies, including
foreign ADRs. It reconstitutes annually and rebalances quarterly. At rebalance, the three largest members
are capped at 12%, 10% and 8%; all remaining members are capped at 4%.

SOXQ therefore combines:

- materially lower cost than SMH, SOXX and XSD;
- sufficient assets and trading volume for this portfolio;
- exposure to designers, memory, foundries and equipment;
- less Nvidia concentration than SMH; and
- higher average business quality than XSD's equal-weight small-company tilt.

SOXX is often mistaken for the investable version of the SOX Index. Despite its ticker, SOXX currently
tracks a different NYSE semiconductor index. SOXQ is the direct PHLX SOX implementation.

## Is SOXQ a Buy Now?

**No—not at a full position.** At approximately $97.83:

- SOXQ is up approximately 100% over one year and 264% over three years;
- it is still about 23% above its 200-day moving average near $79–$80;
- it trades around 40x trailing earnings;
- its three-year beta is approximately 2.27; and
- the industry's fastest growth is concentrated in highly cyclical memory.

The fund is roughly 15% below its 52-week high, but that pullback has not erased the preceding rerating.
The correct response is not to short the sector; it is to avoid chasing it.

## Recommended Implementation

### Now

1. **Do not create a dedicated semiconductor sleeve yet.**
2. Continue funding VTI, VXUS, JQUA and VWO. This builds diversified semiconductor exposure while also
   repairing the portfolio's much larger global-equity deficits.
3. Do not buy AMD, Intel, Micron or a full semiconductor ETF position at current prices.

### On a correction

If the sector corrects without a structural break:

| Trigger | Action |
|---|---|
| SOXQ near approximately $85 | Consider a 0.5% portfolio starter, approximately $3,430 at the current portfolio size |
| SOXQ near its 200-day average around $79–$80 | Permit a second 0.5% tranche |
| AI capex orders weaken, memory prices reverse or earnings estimates fall sharply | Pause; do not buy merely because price declined |

The tactical SOXQ position should initially be capped at **1.0% of the portfolio**. It should replace part
of the intended VTI/JQUA allocation rather than lifting total equity risk. Raising the limit to 1.5% would
require a new review after a broad sector correction or a material earnings catch-up.

If immediate participation is psychologically necessary, cap it at **0.25%** and treat it only as a
tracking position—not as evidence that today's valuation is attractive.

## Bottom Line

Semiconductors are worth owning strategically, and the portfolio will own them automatically through its
core funds. The sector currently combines extraordinary fundamental growth with extraordinary price
performance, valuation and cyclicality. That makes it a poor candidate for a large new allocation today.

**Best plan:** fund the existing broad-core allocations, place SOXQ on the watchlist, and consider a
0.5%–1.0% tactical overweight only after a better entry. AMD and Intel are both included; neither is an
attractive direct purchase now.
