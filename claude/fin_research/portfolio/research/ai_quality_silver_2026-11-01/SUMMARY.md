## Decision

Keep the Quality sleeve at **5.5% JQUA** for now. Do not replace JQUA with the seven direct AI-infrastructure stocks after this decline.

The only direct-stock variant that clears a reasonable quality screen is **4.5% JQUA plus up to 1.0% LRCX**, funded dollar-for-dollar from JQUA. It is an active semiconductor-cycle bet, not a safer version of JQUA. At the 2026-10-30 price, even LRCX is not sufficiently cheap to justify making that change now. KLAC is the second watch candidate. ANET is a superb company at an excessive valuation. ARM, MRVL, LITE, and COHR currently fail the combination of valuation, cash economics, dilution, or concentration required for this sleeve.

Do not add incremental silver while the portfolio still owns 154 WPM shares. At $116.58, WPM is approximately 2.66% of the portfolio. Applying WPM's latest Q2 revenue mix gives an **illustrative 1.38% silver look-through** and 1.22% gold look-through. Revenue mix is not exact metal beta, but it shows that adding a new silver vehicle would increase an existing economic overweight rather than fill the 1% Silver/Platinum target.

Silver is **beaten down, but “cheap” is not established**. On the secondary futures series, the October 30 silver close of $49.10 was only 4.2% above its one-year closing low after a very large spike and reversal. Gold also closed at its one-year low, and the gold/silver ratio was about 79.4. This supports a better entry than the recent peak, but it is not a silver-specific valuation signal. Silver has no earnings anchor and can decline with industrial demand in a recession, so the existing 1% sleeve cap remains appropriate for the portfolio's recession and house-liquidity priorities.

When a clean silver allocation is needed, use **SIVR for long-term Ameritrade exposure**. Use **SLV only when the position is explicitly tactical or options/liquidity matter**. OneGold is reasonable for a minority long-term custody allocation after comparing its live buy/sell spread. PSLV is not the default for a small U.S. taxable position because it is a PFIC and requires a timely QEF election and annual Form 8621 to preserve favorable treatment.

## Why JQUA wins this comparison

JQUA is not free of AI or technology risk. Its official 2026-10-30 holdings were 42.5% technology by the secondary fund data, with AMD, AVGO, NVDA, MU, LRCX, and KLAC already represented. LRCX was 0.80% of JQUA and KLAC 0.68%; the other five candidates were absent. At a 5.5% portfolio allocation, those two holdings contribute only about 0.044% and 0.037% of the total portfolio, respectively.

That small embedded exposure is useful. The household already has Adobe employment and RSU exposure, so a concentrated AI-capex basket would add to the same pro-cyclical source of income and wealth. JQUA spreads the sleeve across 290 common stocks and limits its top ten to 18.68%. The direct names would increase the chance that a semiconductor-capex reversal, hyperscaler spending change, China restriction, or customer loss hits the portfolio during the possible two-to-three-year house-purchase window.

JQUA's current P/E was approximately 23.7 and its fee was 0.12%. The P/E is a secondary estimate and is not perfectly comparable to the single-quarter annualized multiples below, but it shows the valuation hurdle: most direct candidates cost substantially more despite carrying more idiosyncratic risk.

## Do these names solve the Google concern?

They replace Google's business-model risk with AI-capex-cycle risk; they do not remove risk. Alphabet's latest primary results also do not support the claim that Search is already failing: Q3 revenue rose 25%, Google Search and other revenue rose 12%, Search query growth was the highest in five years, and GAAP operating margin was 31.8%. At $279.84, the latest-quarter annualized GAAP P/E was about 33x and the secondary forward estimate was 18.4x. Most of the proposed suppliers were more expensive on confirmed earnings.

The real Alphabet concern is forward-looking. TTM operating cash flow was $179.6B, but $159.8B of capital spending reduced TTM free cash flow to $19.7B. Search economics could deteriorate as user behavior and monetization change. Those are valid reasons to avoid a direct GOOGL position when conviction is low. They are not evidence that ARM at 206x current-quarter GAAP earnings, ANET at 17.5x annualized quarterly sales, or cash-burning optics suppliers are safer substitutes.

JQUA is the coherent middle ground. It owns GOOGL at only 1.47%, so a 5.5% JQUA sleeve contributes approximately 0.081% GOOGL exposure to the whole portfolio. It keeps diversified participation in whichever AI business models win without requiring a concentrated prediction about Search or one supplier chain.

Microsoft is the strongest broad-platform company in the screenshot. Its latest revenue rose 19%, GAAP operating margin was 49.7%, quarterly free cash flow was $18.6B after $39.4B of capital spending, and the latest-quarter annualized GAAP P/E was about 26.5x. This is a much cleaner quality profile than the optics names and a less demanding confirmed-earnings valuation than ANET, ARM, MRVL, or LITE. JQUA already owns MSFT at 2.06%, and direct MSFT would increase the household's software/AI concentration. Therefore it is a valid company to own but does not improve this portfolio enough to replace JQUA.

For a clean 1% silver exposure on the current portfolio, the capital target is about **$6,748**. The existing WPM position has about **$9,336** of illustrative silver revenue look-through. At the same price and revenue mix, about **111 WPM shares** would correspond to 1% silver look-through; 154 shares are roughly 43 shares above that illustrative level. That calculation does not turn WPM into bullion: WPM remains a company with equity-market, counterparty, mine, and management risk.

There are two coherent future implementations:

1. Keep WPM as the precious-metals equity and do not add SIVR. Continue counting its economic silver and gold exposure when checking total portfolio risk.
2. Reduce or exit WPM and replace the direct-metal portion with SIVR. If the existing combined Silver/Platinum policy remains unchanged, the previously validated clean implementation is 0.75% SIVR plus 0.25% PPLT. If the policy is deliberately changed to silver only, use up to 1.0% SIVR. A policy change should precede the trade.

SIVR and SLV are grantor trusts holding bullion, so U.S. taxable gains generally receive collectibles treatment rather than the ordinary 15%/20% long-term stock capital-gains rates. PSLV can preserve long-term capital-gains treatment only if its PFIC/QEF requirements are handled correctly. California adds its normal state tax treatment. Tax administration can therefore matter more than a 0.20 percentage-point fee difference, especially for a short holding period.

## Contrary case and monitoring triggers

The strongest contrary case for buying now is LRCX: revenue growth, margin, net cash, and share shrinkage are all real, while its secondary forward P/E is only 19.5x. If AI memory and leading-edge fab spending persist, waiting for $205 may miss the entry. The portfolio can accept that outcome because JQUA already owns LRCX, KLAC, AMD, AVGO, NVDA, and MU, and the house/job-security objective makes avoiding an additional cyclical bet more valuable than capturing every upside scenario.

Change the recommendation if any of these occur:

- LRCX or KLAC reaches the valuation gate without an earnings cut.
- Their reported backlog, service revenue, China exposure, or share count materially worsens.
- Adobe/RSU concentration falls enough that the household can tolerate a larger AI-capex bet.
- The house-purchase horizon lengthens beyond five years.
- WPM is reduced or reclassified, creating actual room for direct silver.
- SIVR's voluntary fee waiver ends; compare its then-current 0.45% gross cost with SLV and OneGold.
