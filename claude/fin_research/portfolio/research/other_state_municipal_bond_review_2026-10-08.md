# Other-State and National Municipal Bonds — 2026-10-08

## Decision Summary

**Status:** Research complete for the representative funds reviewed. Hooman confirmed on 2026-10-08
that the full 5% target remains in VGIT; no municipal allocation, holding, order or monitoring-script
change was made.

**Conclusion:** No reviewed other-state, national or U.S.-territory municipal option both (1) beats
VTEC after California tax and (2) improves recession protection relative to VGIT.

- **VTEB is the best national-municipal implementation reviewed:** 4.32% 30-day SEC yield, 0.03%
  expense ratio, approximately 7.2-year duration and 10,722 securities. It materially improves
  state and issuer diversification versus VTEC.
- California taxes VTEB's predominantly out-of-state municipal income. Its estimated after-California-
  tax yield is therefore approximately **3.83%-3.88%** under the conservative assumption that the full
  distribution is out-of-state, below VTEC's **4.06%** tax-free yield. The actual annual sponsor-reported
  California-source portion would be exempt and would raise VTEB's result slightly, but not enough to
  overturn the conclusion at current yields.
- National diversification removes much of VTEC's single-state concentration, but it does **not**
  remove municipal-market liquidity, spread, call or recession risk. VTEB lost approximately 17.0%
  during the March 2020 municipal-liquidity shock versus approximately 2.3% for VGIT.
- No current U.S.-listed Texas-only municipal ETF was identified in the major-provider lineups
  reviewed. A direct Texas or other out-of-state bond must yield approximately **4.53%-4.58%** just
  to equal VTEC after California tax. A bond clearing that hurdle is likely to do so through longer
  duration, weaker credit, call complexity or poorer liquidity rather than a free tax advantage.
- U.S.-territory bonds can receive favorable state-tax treatment, but concentrated Puerto Rico or
  other territory credit is not appropriate for the recession-hedge role.

## Texas and Direct Other-State Bonds

### Tax hurdle

California taxes a California resident's Texas municipal interest even though it remains federally
tax-exempt. The yield required to match VTEC is therefore:

```text
At a 10.3% CA rate: 4.06% / (1 - 0.103) = 4.53%
At an 11.3% CA rate: 4.06% / (1 - 0.113) = 4.58%
```

The out-of-state bond only needs approximately 3.40%-3.44% to match VGIT's modeled 3.05% after-tax
income, but income is not the only objective. It also gives up Treasury credit and crisis behavior.

### Product and implementation finding

- **RESEARCH FINDING:** no current U.S.-listed Texas-only municipal ETF was found in the Vanguard,
  iShares and representative major municipal-ETF lineups reviewed. This is economically unsurprising:
  Texas has no individual state income tax, so local investors do not receive a Texas-income-tax
  benefit from a Texas-only vehicle.
- A **single direct Texas bond is less diversified**, not more diversified, than VTEC's 3,917-bond
  portfolio. A ladder across multiple Texas issuers can improve that, but requires larger position
  sizes, credit work and retail execution.
- Texas state and many guaranteed school credits can be very high quality, but high quality generally
  reduces yield. If a live broker quote materially exceeds the 4.53%-4.58% hurdle, inspect duration,
  call date, underlying versus insured/guaranteed rating, tax status, trade size and dealer markup.
- **UNVERIFIED CURRENT INVENTORY:** direct-bond offerings change intraday and broker inventory was not
  available in this research environment. No exact Texas CUSIP or live executable quote should drive
  a trade until checked in the account's bond desk.

### Why another state's headline yield can mislead

An out-of-state bond yielding 4.50% sounds better than VTEC at 4.06%. At an 11.3% California marginal
rate, however, it leaves only 3.99% before sale taxes and transaction costs. The apparent 44-basis-
point advantage becomes a 7-basis-point disadvantage.

Direct bonds can still be useful when a known maturity matches a known liability and the investor can
hold to maturity. Hooman expects possible liquidity needs before maturity and wants recession gains,
so that advantage is less applicable here.

- **IBMS (2030):** 3.43% SEC yield, 3.34-year duration, 0.10% fee.

way to combine state tax exemption with a fixed maturity.

## Territory Exception

Interest from qualifying U.S.-territory obligations can receive federal and state tax exemption,
which can remove California's disadvantage for an out-of-state issuer. This does **not** create a good
recession hedge:

- Puerto Rico's recent restructuring history demonstrates materially higher legal, fiscal and recovery
  risk than Treasury or broad investment-grade municipal exposure.
- Territory markets are smaller and less liquid, and a dedicated position increases rather than
  reduces issuer concentration.
- Higher yield is compensation for credit, structure and liquidity risk. It can behave like risky
  credit during a recession.

**Recommendation:** do not use individual territory bonds or a territory-heavy high-yield muni fund in
the 5% recession-protection sleeve.

## Recession Evidence

**COMPUTED from adjusted daily ETF prices:** from the March 9, 2020 local peak through the municipal-
market trough, VTEB fell approximately **17.0%**, MUB **13.7%**, NYF **13.1%**, and VGIT only **2.3%**.
In 2022's rate shock, VTEB and VGIT both lost roughly 12%-13%, showing that munis can retain ordinary
duration risk while also suffering a separate liquidity event.

National diversification therefore solves the question “what if California alone deteriorates?” It
does not solve “what if investors demand cash and municipal dealers/funds are under stress?” That
second risk is the important one for recession protection.

## Recommendation

1. **Do not replace VGIT with another-state bonds.** Texas, New York and other state exposure loses the
   California exemption without improving Treasury-like recession behavior.
2. **If state concentration is the dominant concern, VTEB is the best reviewed municipal alternative
   to VTEC.** The cost of diversification is approximately 0.18-0.23 percentage point of annual
   after-tax yield at current figures.
3. **If recession protection is the dominant concern, keep VGIT.** Neither VTEC nor VTEB should be
   described as a Treasury substitute.
4. **Do not use territory/high-yield municipal debt for this sleeve.** It may increase income, but it
   moves the portfolio farther from the stated objective.
5. **Under the current recession-protection objective, keep the accepted 5% in VGIT.** If a separate
   tax-efficient-income sleeve is later approved, cap an initial municipal position at 1.0%-1.5% and
   fund it separately rather than weakening the 5% hedge. Choose VTEC for maximum after-tax carry;
   choose VTEB only if sacrificing roughly 0.2% yield is worth reducing California concentration.
