# North American Energy-Export Thesis — 2026-10-04

## Decision Summary

Hooman's intuition contains a strong investable core, but the geopolitical framing is too absolute.

- **Strong version:** North America is becoming the indispensable *marginal* supplier of LNG and an
  increasingly important exporter of crude oil, refined products and natural-gas liquids. Europe and
  Asia will pay for secure, flexible supply, while U.S. and Canadian infrastructure earns fees on the
  growing volumes. **Conviction: 8/10 structurally over 5–10 years.**
- **Overstated version:** the United States will control South American and Middle Eastern production,
  Russia will go offline, and other countries will have no choice but to buy U.S. energy. **Conviction:
  3/10.** Russia remains a major producer, Brazil and Guyana are independent growth sources, OPEC+
  retains substantial production and pricing power, and Europe is deliberately diversifying among
  LNG, Norway, North Africa, Azerbaijan, renewables, storage and demand reduction.
- **Petrodollar version:** energy exports help U.S. trade, diplomacy and strategic influence, but they
  are not a replacement for the foundations of dollar dominance: deep U.S. capital markets, abundant
  safe assets, dollar liabilities, trade invoicing, legal institutions and network effects.
  **Conviction: 2/10 as the primary mechanism preserving dollar hegemony.**

The best portfolio expression is **export infrastructure rather than more raw commodity exposure**.
Hooman already has substantial producer and natural-gas exposure but almost no LNG terminal or
midstream exposure. If this thesis is later approved as a portfolio allocation, the preferred balanced
implementation is **Cheniere (`LNG`) plus `MLPX`**, funded primarily by rotating existing energy risk,
not by immediately increasing the aggregate energy allocation.

This review does **not** change the live allocation. Sizing and funding remain proposals until Hooman
confirms them.

## The Hard Data

### U.S. export capacity is expanding rapidly

EIA's September 2026 Short-Term Energy Outlook gives the following annual averages:

| Measure | 2024 | 2025 | 2026E | 2027E |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| U.S. dry natural-gas production, Bcf/d | 103.08 | 107.62 | 111.69 | 115.89 |
| U.S. LNG gross exports, Bcf/d | 11.93 | 15.09 | 17.38 | 18.62 |
| U.S. crude production, mb/d | 13.27 | 13.66 | 13.83 | 14.26 |
| U.S. petroleum-product net imports, mb/d | -4.77 | -4.98 | -6.07 | -5.56 |
| U.S. total-liquids net imports, mb/d | -2.27 | -2.80 | -4.24 | -3.81 |

Negative net imports mean net exports. U.S. LNG exports are forecast to rise approximately **56% from
2024 through 2027**, while petroleum-product exports remain structurally large.

The critical nuance is that U.S. gas production rises almost as aggressively as LNG exports. EIA's
Henry Hub annual forecast declines from **$3.53/MMBtu in 2025 to $3.43 in 2026 and $3.28 in 2027**.
Therefore, the export-volume thesis does not automatically imply a rising domestic gas price. It is a
much cleaner thesis for contracted liquefaction and pipeline operators than for `UNG`.

### North America gains share, but not a monopoly

EIA's forecast total-liquids production averages approximately:

| Producer | 2025 | 2026E | 2027E |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| United States | 23.68 mb/d | 24.31 mb/d | 25.13 mb/d |
| Canada | 6.26 mb/d | 6.38 mb/d | 6.47 mb/d |
| Brazil | 4.65 mb/d | 5.25 mb/d | 5.33 mb/d |
| Guyana | 0.75 mb/d | 0.92 mb/d | 1.15 mb/d |
| Russia | 10.53 mb/d | 10.44 mb/d | 10.57 mb/d |

This supports North American scale and Exxon's Guyana opportunity, but it also shows why “U.S.
control” is the wrong model. Competing sovereign producers remain large, and South American growth
does not belong to the United States merely because U.S. companies participate.

### The LNG buildout is structural, but later-decade competition is real

IEA's *Gas 2025* expected roughly **300 bcm/year of new global LNG export capacity by 2030**, led by
the United States and Qatar. More than 70% of additions were expected from those two countries, and
the U.S. could provide one-third of global LNG supply by decade-end. IEA's Q3-2026 update says Middle
East damage may delay part of the supply wave in 2026–27, while three additional U.S. projects reached
final investment decision in 2026.

That creates two different phases:

1. **2026–27:** unusually tight global gas balances and impaired Middle Eastern supply favor existing,
   operating U.S. exporters.
2. **2028–30:** large U.S., Qatari and other projects increase supply and can compress merchant LNG
   margins. Contracted operators remain better protected than uncontracted developers or gas futures.

### Why Cheniere is the best pure play

Cheniere now operates approximately **56 mtpa** of LNG capacity after Corpus Christi Stage 3, more
than 20% above its prior platform. It exported 371 cargoes in the first half of 2026 and raised 2026
guidance to **$7.9–$8.4B adjusted EBITDA** and **$5.3–$5.8B distributable cash flow**.

Against a roughly $55.8B market capitalization, that guidance implies an approximate **9.5%–10.4%
distributable-cash-flow yield** before considering debt and minority interests. The company had $23B
of debt at year-end 2025, so it is not low-risk, but the contract structure is unusually resilient:

- customers generally pay a fixed liquefaction fee whether or not they lift the cargo;
- the variable fee is generally 115% of Henry Hub and is intended to cover feedgas and fuel; and
- only the uncontracted/marketing volumes carry substantial spot-market exposure.

This is closer to a contracted export franchise than a simple bet on U.S. gas prices. Cheniere reports
Q3 results on **2026-10-29**; that is the next clean checkpoint for guidance, Stage 3 utilization,
capital returns and post-conflict merchant margins.

### Why MLPX is the best ETF

`MLPX` is not a pure LNG ETF. That is a feature for this portfolio: it spreads project, regulatory and
counterparty risk across the pipelines, gathering systems and terminals needed to move North American
energy. Its August 2026 holdings included:

- Williams 7.73%;
- TC Energy 7.34%;
- Enbridge 7.20%;
- Kinder Morgan 6.87%;
- ONEOK 6.56%;
- Targa 6.34%;
- Cheniere 4.84%; and
- smaller positions in MPLX and Energy Transfer.

The 0.45% fee is higher than a broad energy ETF, but the exposure is materially different. `MLPX`
limits direct MLP ownership to preserve a tax-efficient fund structure and avoid the corporate-level
tax drag found in MLP-heavy C-corporation funds such as `AMLP`. `AMLP` currently reports 1.01% total
operating expenses, including estimated income-tax expense, and is therefore not the preferred core.

`ENFR` is a legitimate alternative at 0.35%. Choose `MLPX` for larger assets, somewhat more explicit
gas-transport exposure and the cleanest match to this thesis. Choose `ENFR` only if the 0.10-point fee
saving matters more than those implementation differences.

Neither is bond-like. Over the last decade, historical maximum drawdowns were approximately 63%–65%
because midstream equities collapsed during 2020. “Fee based” means less commodity sensitivity, not
principal safety.

### Why Tourmaline is the best Canadian satellite

Tourmaline is already owned. Its July 2026 presentation shows:

- 140 MMcf/d of 15-year JKM-linked exposure through Cheniere beginning in 2023;
- additional TTF-linked contracts with Trafigura, Hartree, EDF, Uniper and Centrica;
- international pricing exposure expected to reach 332,500 MMBtu/d by 2028–30; and
- a low-cost, low-debt operating model.

It is a better long-horizon expression than adding more `UNG`, but it remains a producer. It should be
funded inside the existing producer/natural-gas budget rather than stacked on top of it.

## What Would Invalidate The Thesis?

- U.S. LNG projects suffer persistent utilization or permitting problems rather than temporary delays.
- Long-term LNG contracts are signed at materially weaker fixed fees, or buyers successfully escape
  take-or-pay obligations.
- European and Asian gas demand falls faster than expected as renewables, storage, efficiency and
  alternative suppliers absorb the security premium.
- The 2028–30 LNG supply wave drives global prices toward marginal U.S. export cost for a sustained
  period, eliminating merchant economics and delaying new final investment decisions.
- U.S. feedgas prices rise enough to impair LNG competitiveness or trigger durable political export
  restrictions.
- Russia and Middle Eastern export infrastructure recover more quickly than expected while new Qatar
  capacity arrives on time.
- Midstream companies overbuild, lever balance sheets excessively or fail to secure firm contracts.
