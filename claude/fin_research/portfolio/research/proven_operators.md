## 0. The reframe (read this first — it matters)

Hooman's instinct: *"people/leaders with a high track record of success are more likely to succeed
again — a good investment strategy. E.g. I've heard companies connected to Jewish investors or
Israeli founders generally do well."*

**The underlying instinct is sound and evidence-backed. The ethnic/religious framing is not, and I've
deliberately dropped it.** Two reasons, stated plainly and respectfully:

1. **It isn't a real signal — it's confounding.** Any apparent correlation between an ethnic/religious
   group and business success is a proxy for other things (education, immigrant selection effects,
   professional networks, industry concentration, base rates in finance/tech hubs). It has **zero
   predictive power at the level of an individual company's future cash flows**, which is what you're
   actually buying. Screening on it would add noise, not edge — and it's neither a defensible nor an
   appropriate investment criterion.
2. **The defensible version of your instinct is much stronger:** *invest alongside proven,
   high-integrity **capital allocators** and **founder-operators** with demonstrated, multi-decade,
   per-share track records of compounding shareholder value, and meaningful skin in the game.* That is
   a real, well-studied factor (see §1). It captures everything true in your instinct — proven winners,
   aligned incentives, long-term orientation — with none of the spurious part.

So this report hunts for **the person/structure behind the compounding**, then asks Hooman's usual
second question: *is it also reasonably priced today?* (quality-at-a-PRICE, not quality at any price).

## 1. Is "jockey investing" actually evidence-backed?

**Short answer: yes, moderately — with a large survivorship-bias asterisk you must respect.**

- **"The Outsiders" (William Thorndike, 2012).** Profiles 8 CEOs who beat the S&P 500 (and Jack Welch's
  GE) over their tenures through **rational capital allocation** — decentralized ops, opportunistic
  buybacks at low prices, disciplined M&A, tax-efficient structures — rather than operational charisma.
  The 8: **Tom Murphy (Capital Cities), Henry Singleton (Teledyne), Bill Anders (General Dynamics),
  John Malone (TCI), Katharine Graham (Washington Post), Bill Stiritz (Ralston), Dick Smith (General
  Cinema), Warren Buffett (Berkshire).** The group **"outperformed the S&P 500 by over twenty times and
  their peers by over seven times"** (VERIFIED, book text); Welch's GE beat the S&P ~3.3× over his tenure
  — so the Outsiders beat Welch by ~7×. Individual annualized returns clustered ~16–30%/yr (Malone ~30%,
  Singleton/Buffett/Stiritz/Murphy ~20%). **VERIFIED** framework + group stats; per-CEO CAGRs directional.
- **Founder-CEO outperformance (Rüdiger Fahlenbrach, "Founder-CEOs, Investment Decisions, and Stock
  Market Performance," *JFQA* 44(2):439–466, 2009 — the strongest single, peer-reviewed anchor).** A
  portfolio of founder-CEO firms (1993–2002) earned **~+4.4%/yr statistically-significant abnormal return**
  after controls (8.3% equal-wt / 10.7% value-wt benchmark-adjusted); founder-CEO firms (~11% of largest
  US firms) invested more in R&D/capex, had **~+31% more patent citations**, more focused M&A. **VERIFIED.**
- **Bain & Co "The Founder's Mentality" (Zook & Allen, 2016):** S&P 500 firms still led by founders
  delivered TSR **~3.1× higher** than other S&P 500 firms over **1990–2014**; ~2/3 of sustained value
  creators show the "founder's mentality." **VERIFIED** (Bain / *Fortune* Jul 2016). Consulting research,
  descriptive not risk-adjusted.
- **Credit Suisse "Family 1000" (2020):** family/founder-owned firms outperformed non-family peers by
  **~300–400 bps/yr since 2006**. **VERIFIED.**
- **The "~3×" headline stat — attribution corrected:** the widely-repeated **"founder-led 170% vs 56% over
  5 years" is journalism** — *USA Today* (Krantz, Mar 2016) citing Prof. Shaker Zahra (U. Minnesota) — NOT
  a Purdue/Shulman study. Directionally consistent with the above; don't attach a "%/yr" to it. **VERIFIED.**

**The honest caveat (survivorship / selection bias):** these studies condition on firms that *survived
and grew*. For every Buffett there are dozens of "visionary founders" who blew up (see: most SPACs, WeWork,
and the 60–190× founder-hype names). Founder control also entrenches governance risk — a great allocator's
super-voting stock is a disaster if the next generation is mediocre (see the Constellation/HEICO/Loews
succession flags in §3). **The factor is real but noisy; the discipline is to demand a *long, already-realized*
track record AND a *reasonable price* — which filters out the survivorship trap and the hype names.**
