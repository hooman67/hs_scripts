**Verdict on your premise:** *Half right.* The archetype ("mature, commoditized, no-moat auto

**a stock being expensive is NOT a short thesis by itself** — is the single most important lesson in
this whole report. Expensive things stay expensive; you need *deterioration + a catalyst.*

## 1. What actually makes an "obvious" short (the bar)

A good short needs **all four**, not just #1:

1. **Expensive / unjustified valuation** — high P/E or P/S with no growth, or negative earnings at a rich multiple.
2. **No moat / commoditized / structurally declining** — the business is genuinely getting worse, not just cyclically soft.
3. **Deteriorating fundamentals** — falling revenue/margins, rising leverage, cash burn, dilution.
4. **A catalyst** — a *reason it de-rates on a clock* (debt maturity, guidance cut, dilution, credit event, litigation, lock-up). Without #4 you are just renting a "value trap" and paying borrow to wait.

**Corollary risk (why most retail shorts lose):** the crowded, obviously-bad names are *already
heavily shorted* — which is exactly what fuels short squeezes. The better the "obvious short"
looks, the more likely it's a squeeze trap. Every name below carries a squeeze-risk grade.

## 6. ⚠️ REALITY CHECK — Should *you* short individual stocks at all? (read this)

**Honest bottom line: probably not, or only in tiny, defined-risk size (long puts) as pure
entertainment/education money you can lose entirely.** Here's the unvarnished case against, and
why it matters *specifically for you*.

### 6.1 The structural math is stacked against a short-seller

1. **Unlimited loss, capped gain.** A long can only go to zero (−100%). A short's loss is
   *unbounded* — a stock that 5x's costs you 400% on that position. Your best case is +100% (goes
   to zero); your worst case is arbitrarily large. **The payoff is exactly backwards from what you
   want** for a defensive, house-saving book.

2. **Negative expected drift.** Equity markets rise over time (equity risk premium). A short is a
   bet *against* that structural tailwind — you are swimming upstream every single day. Index
   longs get paid to wait; shorts *pay* to wait.

3. **You pay carry to hold.** Borrow fees (cost-to-borrow) run from ~0.3% (RBLX, AFRM, AMC) to
   **~16%/yr (LCID)** on the hard-to-borrow names — the very names with the best fundamental short
   thesis are the most expensive to borrow. You also owe any dividends. On a name yielding 8.9%
   (MNRO), you pay that out of pocket. **Carry alone can turn a "right" thesis into a losing trade.**

4. **Recall risk.** On locked-float names (LCID — PIF owns ~60% and won't lend), your broker can
   *recall* the borrow and force you to cover at the worst possible moment.

### 6.2 The behavioral math is even worse

5. **"The market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent."** Every "obviously
   expensive" name here (VVV at 42x, HOOD at 19x sales) can stay expensive for *years*. Being right
   on valuation and wrong on timing is indistinguishable from being wrong.

6. **Short squeezes.** The crowded, obviously-bad names are heavily shorted *because* they're
   obviously bad — which is the fuel. GME (17 days-to-cover), LCID (33–38% SI), KSS (27%, proven
   squeezer July 2025), BYND (26%), PLUG (21%) can rip 30–75% *in a day* on no fundamental news.
   Keith Gill's single 2024 tweet moved GME +74%. **A squeeze can margin-call you out of a
   position that's completely right on the fundamentals.**

7. **Catalysts cut both ways.** Every catalyst above (earnings, M&A, guidance) is a *binary event*
   — and the "bad" companies are so beaten down that a *less-bad* print gaps them up. Macy's raised
   guidance; PSKY raised EBITDA guidance; PLUG is raising guidance. The obvious-short crowd gets
   run over on "not as bad as feared."

8. **Win-rate reality.** Even professional short-sellers with teams, data, and prime-broker borrow
   run **low hit-rates** and depend on a few big winners to offset many small carry-bleeding losses.
   Famous shorts (Enron, Wirecard, Luckin) are survivorship-biased memories; the graveyard of
   correct-but-early shorts who blew up first is much larger and unremembered. Dedicated short funds
   have largely closed over the past decade *because the strategy structurally underperforms.*

### 6.3 Why it especially doesn't fit *your* situation

- **You're a Canadian investor with a house goal and a cash-heavy, defensive book.** Shorting adds
  *unlimited-downside, path-dependent, margin-callable* risk to a portfolio whose entire job is
  capital preservation for a near-term real-life goal. That's a category error. A cash-heavy
  defensive book and a short book are opposite risk postures.
- **Tax/account friction (Canada):** short sales generate ordinary-income treatment (not capital
  gains) in a taxable account, cannot be done in registered accounts (RRSP/TFSA), require a margin
  account, and dividends paid on shorts are not deductible the way you'd hope. It's tax-inefficient
  on top of everything else.
- **Opportunity cost:** the effort to research, time, and babysit a short (borrow, recall, squeeze,
  earnings) is enormous versus simply *not owning* the bad company, or trimming the overvalued
  longs you already flagged (CRCL, RBLX, LCID) — which captures most of the "I think this is
  overvalued" view with **none** of the unlimited-loss/squeeze/carry downside.

### 6.4 If you still want exposure — the least-bad way

- **Express bearishness by NOT owning / by exiting**, not by shorting. You already flagged CRCL,
  RBLX, LCID as EXIT — *that is the trade.* Selling an overvalued holding captures the view risk-free.
- **If you insist on active shorts, use long puts, not short stock.** A put caps your loss at the
  premium (defined risk), needs no borrow, can't be recalled, and can't be squeezed out of. Buy
  time (dated past the catalyst). Size it as money you're fully prepared to lose to zero.
- **Prefer names with a hard catalyst on a clock + low squeeze risk + cheap borrow** — from this
  report that's **RBLX** (guided bookings decline, 0.41% borrow, 3.4% SI) and **CRCL** (rate-driven,
  liquid). **Avoid** the high-SI cult names (GME, LCID, KSS, BYND, PLUG, CVNA) as naked shorts
  entirely — the fundamentals are best there and the mechanics are lethal.
- **Cap total short exposure at a tiny % of the book** (a satellite of a satellite), ring-fenced
  from the house money.

**One-line summary:** *Your instinct that bad companies are easy to spot is correct. But "easy to
spot as bad" and "profitable to short" are almost unrelated — shorting is a low-win-rate,
unlimited-loss, carry-bleeding, squeeze-prone game that fights the market's upward drift, and it's a
poor fit for a Canadian saver protecting a house down-payment. The highest-expected-value expression
of your bearish views is to simply not own (or to exit) the overvalued names — which you're already
doing.*
